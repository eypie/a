Solid State Physics: Unit-II

Solved Questions — Band Theory & Semiconductors
1. If the energy of an electron in a band is given by \(E = A + Bk + Ck^2\), calculate the effective mass of an electron.

Explanation: The effective mass (\(m^*\)) is determined by how "curvy" the energy-momentum (\(E-k\)) graph is. Mathematically, it relates to the second derivative of Energy with respect to the wave vector \(k\).

Solution: The formula for effective mass is:

\[ m^* = \frac{\hbar^2}{\frac{d^2E}{dk^2}} \]

1. First Derivative:

\[ \frac{dE}{dk} = \frac{d}{dk}(A + Bk + Ck^2) = B + 2Ck \]

2. Second Derivative:

\[ \frac{d^2E}{dk^2} = \frac{d}{dk}(B + 2Ck) = 2C \]

3. Substitute into the formula:

\[ m^* = \frac{\hbar^2}{2C} \]
2. In a Cu specimen, the drift velocity of an electron in an electric field of 500 V/m is 2.17 m/s. Determine (i) mobility (ii) relaxation time for electrons.

Explanation: Mobility measures how easily electrons move under an electric field. Relaxation time is the average time between collisions.

Given: \(E = 500\ \text{V/m}\), \(v_d = 2.17\ \text{m/s}\), \(m = 9.1 \times 10^{-31}\ \text{kg}\), \(e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}\).

(i) Mobility (\(\mu\)):

\[ \mu = \frac{v_d}{E} = \frac{2.17}{500} = 4.34 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m}^2\text{V}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1} \]

(ii) Relaxation Time (\(\tau\)):
We know \(\mu = \frac{e\tau}{m}\), so:

\[ \tau = \frac{\mu m}{e} = \frac{(4.34 \times 10^{-3}) \times (9.1 \times 10^{-31})}{1.6 \times 10^{-19}} \]
\[ \tau \approx 2.47 \times 10^{-14}\ \text{seconds} \]
3. What is Hall effect? Obtain an expression for Hall coefficient in metals.

Explanation: When you place a current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field that is perpendicular to the current, a voltage is generated across the conductor. This is the Hall Effect. It happens because the magnetic field pushes the moving electrons to one side of the material.

Derivation:

1. In equilibrium, the Magnetic Force (\(F_m\)) equals the Electric Force (\(F_e\)):

\[ eE_H = ev_dB \quad \Rightarrow \quad E_H = v_dB \]

2. Since current density \(J = nev_d\), we have \(v_d = \frac{J}{ne}\).

3. Substitute \(v_d\):

\[ E_H = \frac{J B}{ne} \]

4. The Hall Coefficient (\(R_H\)) is defined as the Hall field per unit current density per unit magnetic field:

\[ R_H = \frac{E_H}{JB} = \frac{1}{ne} \]

The sign of \(R_H\) indicates the type of charge carriers (negative for electrons, positive for holes).

4. What do you mean by forbidden band?

Definition: A Forbidden Band (or Energy Gap, \(E_g\)) is a range of energy levels in a solid where no electron states can exist.

Easy wording: Think of it like a "no-man's land" between the valence band and the conduction band. Electrons must gain enough energy to "jump" across this gap to conduct electricity.

Key points:
  • Electrons cannot stay in the forbidden band.
  • The size of the forbidden band determines whether a material is a conductor, semiconductor, or insulator.
  • In semiconductors, the forbidden band is small (~1 eV), so electrons can jump across with thermal energy.
5. What do you mean by effective mass of electron?

Definition: The Effective Mass (\(m^*\))** is the mass that an electron seems to have when it is moving inside a crystal lattice.

Why it exists: Inside a solid, an electron isn't just moving in a vacuum; it's being pushed and pulled by the atoms in the lattice. This makes it accelerate differently than a free electron. We use \(m^*\) to simplify the math so we can still use Newton's Second Law (\(F = m^*a\)).

\[ m^* = \frac{\hbar^2}{\frac{d^2E}{dk^2}} \]
Important points:
  • \(m^*\) can be different from the free electron mass (\(9.1 \times 10^{-31}\) kg).
  • Near the bottom of a band, \(m^*\) is positive.
  • Near the top of a band, \(m^*\) becomes negative (which leads to the concept of holes).
6. Define Relaxation Time.

Definition: Relaxation Time (\(\tau\))** is the average time that passes between two successive collisions of an electron with the lattice ions.

Easy wording: It is the "free time" an electron has to accelerate before it bumps into something and loses its momentum.

\[ \mu = \frac{e\tau}{m} \]
Significance:
  • Larger \(\tau\) means fewer collisions, hence higher mobility and conductivity.
  • Relaxation time decreases with increasing temperature because atoms vibrate more.
  • Typical values are around \(10^{-14}\) seconds for metals at room temperature.
7. How are conductors, insulators and semiconductors differentiated on the basis of energy band theory?

Answer: Based on the energy band theory, materials are differentiated by the size of the band gap (\(E_g\)) and the occupancy of bands.

ExampleCopper, SilverDiamond, GlassSilicon, Germanium
Explanation:
  • Conductors: Valence and conduction bands overlap, so electrons can move freely even at absolute zero.
  • Insulators: Large band gap prevents electrons from jumping to the conduction band at room temperature.
  • Semiconductors: Small band gap allows some electrons to jump to the conduction band with thermal energy at room temperature.
8. State and prove Bloch theorem.

Statement: The wave function of an electron moving in a periodic potential \(V(x)\) of a crystal lattice is a plane wave modulated by a periodic function \(u_k(x)\) having the same periodicity as the lattice.

\[ \psi_k(x) = u_k(x) e^{ikx} \quad \text{where } u_k(x) = u_k(x+a) \]

Proof (Summary):

1. Schrödinger equation for a periodic potential:

\[ -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} + V(x)\psi = E\psi \]

2. Because the potential is periodic (\(V(x) = V(x+a)\)), the probability density \(|\psi(x)|^2\) must also be periodic: \(|\psi(x+a)|^2 = |\psi(x)|^2\).

3. This implies \(\psi(x+a) = \lambda \psi(x)\), where \(|\lambda|^2 = 1\), so \(\lambda = e^{ika}\).

4. Let us assume a solution of the form \(\psi(x) = u(x)e^{ikx}\). Then:

\[ \psi(x+a) = u(x+a)e^{ik(x+a)} = u(x+a)e^{ikx}e^{ika} \]

5. For this to equal \(e^{ika}\psi(x) = e^{ika}u(x)e^{ikx}\), we must have \(u(x+a) = u(x)\).

6. Therefore, \(\psi_k(x) = u_k(x)e^{ikx}\) with \(u_k(x)\) periodic satisfies the required condition, proving Bloch's theorem.

9. Discuss the Crystal momentum and obtain the expression for the effective mass of electron in the lattice.

Crystal Momentum: Even though an electron in a crystal is not "free," it reacts to external forces as if it has a momentum \(p = \hbar k\). This is called Crystal Momentum. It is not the same as classical momentum because it includes the interaction with the lattice.

Derivation of Effective Mass:

1. Velocity of an electron (group velocity):

\[ v_g = \frac{d\omega}{dk} \]

Since \(E = \hbar\omega\), then:

\[ v_g = \frac{1}{\hbar} \frac{dE}{dk} \]

2. Acceleration (\(a\)):

\[ a = \frac{dv_g}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt} \left( \frac{1}{\hbar} \frac{dE}{dk} \right) = \frac{1}{\hbar} \frac{d^2E}{dk^2} \frac{dk}{dt} \]

3. Force (\(F\)) from external field relates to crystal momentum:

\[ F = \hbar \frac{dk}{dt} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \frac{dk}{dt} = \frac{F}{\hbar} \]

4. Substitute \(\frac{dk}{dt}\) into the acceleration equation:

\[ a = \frac{1}{\hbar} \frac{d^2E}{dk^2} \left( \frac{F}{\hbar} \right) = \frac{F}{\hbar^2} \frac{d^2E}{dk^2} \]

5. Rearranging to find \(F\):

\[ F = \left[ \frac{\hbar^2}{d^2E/dk^2} \right] a \]

6. Comparing with Newton's second law \(F = m^*a\), we get the effective mass:

\[ m^* = \frac{\hbar^2}{\frac{d^2E}{dk^2}} \]

Physical Interpretation: The effective mass depends on the curvature of the \(E-k\) diagram. Large curvature (steep parabola) → small \(m^*\); small curvature (flat band) → large \(m^*\).
