HSAB — Electronegativity, Hardness, Softness & Theories
3½ + 3½ marks
Part A
Establish a relationship between electronegativity of a species with its hardness and softness.

What is Electronegativity?

Electronegativity is the ability of an atom or ion to attract electrons toward itself. Higher electronegativity means the species holds electrons tightly.

Relationship with Hard and Soft Species

Hard acids/bases have high electronegativity, small size, and high charge density. They hold their electrons tightly, so they are not easily polarised (non-polarisable). Examples: \( \text{H}^+, \text{Li}^+, \text{Mg}^{2+}, \text{F}^-, \text{OH}^- \)

Soft acids/bases have low electronegativity, large size, and low charge density. Their electrons are loosely held, so they are easily polarised. Examples: \( \text{Cu}^+, \text{Ag}^+, \text{I}^-, \text{S}^{2-} \)

Key Rule: High electronegativity → Tight electron hold → Hard species. Low electronegativity → Loose electron hold → Soft species.

PropertyHardSoft
ElectronegativityHighLow
PolarisabilityLowHigh
SizeSmallLarge
ChargeHigh (+)Low or zero
Part B
Explain various theories related with the HSAB concept.

1. Pearson's HSAB Principle (1963)

R.G. Pearson classified acids and bases as Hard, Soft, or Borderline. The principle states: "Hard acids prefer to bind with hard bases, and soft acids prefer to bind with soft bases."

2. Klopman's Frontier Orbital Theory

This theory explains HSAB using HOMO (Highest Occupied MO) and LUMO (Lowest Unoccupied MO) energies. Soft–soft interactions are orbital-controlled (covalent), while hard–hard interactions are charge-controlled (electrostatic/ionic).

3. Drago–Wayland Model (E & C Equation)

This model calculates the enthalpy of acid–base interaction using:

\[ -\Delta H = E_A E_B + C_A C_B \]

Where \(E\) = electrostatic contribution and \(C\) = covalent contribution. This gives a quantitative measure of stability.

4. Absolute Hardness (Pearson, 1988)

Hardness \(\eta\) is defined as:

\[ \eta = \frac{IP - EA}{2} \]

Where IP = Ionisation Potential, EA = Electron Affinity. High \(\eta\) → Hard; Low \(\eta\) → Soft.

Symbiosis · Characteristics of Hard & Soft Acids/Bases
3 + 4 marks
Part A
Explain symbiosis with examples.

What is Symbiosis?

The term symbiosis (introduced by Jørgensen) in HSAB context means: when multiple soft ligands are attached to a metal, the metal becomes softer, and vice-versa for hard ligands.

In short: "Like ligands reinforce each other's effect on the metal centre."

Examples

\( \text{BF}_3 \) — Three hard F⁻ ligands make B harder → it prefers other hard donors.

\( \text{B(CH}_3)_3 \) — Three soft methyl groups make B softer → it prefers soft donors like sulfur-based ligands.

This concept explains why certain combinations of ligands are more stable than mixed ones.

Part B
What are the characteristics of hard and soft acids and bases?

Hard Acids

  • Small size, high positive charge
  • High electronegativity, not easily polarised
  • Outer electrons held tightly
  • Examples: \( \text{H}^+, \text{Li}^+, \text{Na}^+, \text{Mg}^{2+}, \text{Al}^{3+}, \text{BF}_3 \)

Soft Acids

  • Large size, low or zero positive charge
  • Low electronegativity, easily polarised
  • Several outer electrons loosely held
  • Examples: \( \text{Cu}^+, \text{Ag}^+, \text{Hg}^{2+}, \text{Pt}^{2+}, \text{BH}_3 \)

Hard Bases

  • High electronegativity donor atom
  • Low polarisability, hard to oxidise
  • Examples: \( \text{F}^-, \text{OH}^-, \text{H}_2\text{O}, \text{NH}_3, \text{ROH} \)

Soft Bases

  • Low electronegativity donor atom
  • High polarisability, easily oxidised
  • Examples: \( \text{I}^-, \text{S}^{2-}, \text{CN}^-, \text{CO}, \text{PR}_3 \)

Magnetic Behaviour of Transition Metal Complexes · Crystal Field Parameters
3½ + 3 marks
Part A
How many types of magnetic behaviour are found in transition metal complexes? Explain.

Types of Magnetic Behaviour

Magnetic behaviour depends on whether a complex has unpaired electrons or not.

  1. Diamagnetic — All electrons are paired. The substance is weakly repelled by a magnetic field. Example: \([\text{Ni(CN)}_4]^{2-}\), \([\text{Co(NH}_3)_6]^{3+}\)
  2. Paramagnetic — Has one or more unpaired electrons. The substance is attracted to a magnetic field. Example: \([\text{Fe(H}_2\text{O)}_6]^{3+}\)
  3. Ferromagnetic — Unpaired electrons aligned parallel in same direction → very strong attraction. Example: Fe, Co, Ni metals.
  4. Antiferromagnetic — Unpaired electrons aligned anti-parallel → cancel each other → net zero magnetism. Example: MnO
  5. Ferrimagnetic — Unequal anti-parallel spins → small net magnetism. Example: \(\text{Fe}_3\text{O}_4\)

Magnetic Moment Formula: \[ \mu_s = \sqrt{n(n+2)} \text{ B.M.} \] where \(n\) = number of unpaired electrons, B.M. = Bohr Magneton

Part B
What are the factors affecting Crystal Field Parameters? Explain with suitable examples.

Crystal Field Parameter \(\Delta\) (or 10Dq)

The crystal field splitting energy \(\Delta\) depends on several factors:

1. Nature of the Ligand (Spectrochemical Series)

Stronger field ligands cause larger \(\Delta\). Order: \[\text{I}^- < \text{Br}^- < \text{Cl}^- < \text{F}^- < \text{OH}^- < \text{H}_2\text{O} < \text{NH}_3 < \text{en} < \text{CN}^- < \text{CO}\]

Example: \([\text{Co(CN)}_6]^{3-}\) has larger \(\Delta\) than \([\text{CoF}_6]^{3-}\)

2. Charge on the Metal Ion

Higher oxidation state → stronger attraction of ligands → larger \(\Delta\). Example: \(\text{Co}^{3+}\) gives larger \(\Delta\) than \(\text{Co}^{2+}\)

3. Period of the Metal

3rd row > 2nd row > 1st row transition metals. Example: \(\Delta\) increases from Fe → Ru → Os.

4. Geometry of the Complex

\(\Delta_{\text{oct}} > \Delta_{\text{tet}}\). For same metal and ligands: \(\Delta_{\text{tet}} = \frac{4}{9} \Delta_{\text{oct}}\)

Crystal Field Splitting — Octahedral & Tetrahedral · Colour of Complexes
6½ marks

Crystal Field Splitting in Octahedral Complexes

In an octahedral complex, 6 ligands approach along the x, y, and z axes. This causes the five d-orbitals to split into two sets:

  • \(e_g\) set (higher energy): \(d_{z^2}\) and \(d_{x^2-y^2}\) — these point directly at ligands → greater repulsion
  • \(t_{2g}\) set (lower energy): \(d_{xy}, d_{yz}, d_{xz}\) — these point between ligands → less repulsion

Energy difference: \(\Delta_o\) (or 10Dq). The \(e_g\) set is raised by \(+0.6\Delta_o\) and \(t_{2g}\) is lowered by \(-0.4\Delta_o\).

Crystal Field Splitting in Tetrahedral Complexes

In a tetrahedral complex, 4 ligands approach from alternate corners of a cube. The splitting is reversed:

  • \(e\) set (lower energy): \(d_{z^2}\) and \(d_{x^2-y^2}\)
  • \(t_2\) set (higher energy): \(d_{xy}, d_{yz}, d_{xz}\)

\[\Delta_t = \frac{4}{9} \Delta_o\] (tetrahedral splitting is much smaller than octahedral)

How CFT Explains Colour of Complexes

When white light falls on a complex, the metal absorbs photons of a specific energy that matches the \(\Delta\) value. Electrons jump from the lower d-level to the upper d-level (d–d transition). The complementary colour of the absorbed light is what we see.

Example 1: \([\text{Ti(H}_2\text{O)}_6]^{3+}\) absorbs green light (~500 nm) → appears purple/violet

Example 2: \([\text{Cu(H}_2\text{O)}_6]^{2+}\) absorbs red light → appears blue

A complex with no unpaired electrons (all d⁰ or d¹⁰) cannot show d–d transitions → appears colourless. Examples: \(\text{Zn}^{2+}\), \(\text{Sc}^{3+}\)

Kinetic & Thermodynamic Stability · Trans Effect
3½ + 3 marks
Part A
Describe the kinetic and thermodynamic stability of the Complex Compounds.

Thermodynamic Stability

This refers to how stable a complex is at equilibrium. It is measured by the stability constant \(K_f\):

\[ \text{M} + n\text{L} \rightleftharpoons [\text{ML}_n] \quad K_f = \frac{[\text{ML}_n]}{[\text{M}][\text{L}]^n} \]

Higher \(K_f\) → more stable complex. This does NOT tell us how fast the reaction is — only the final state.

Kinetic Stability

This refers to the rate of ligand substitution (how fast a ligand is replaced). Complexes are classified as:

  • Labile: react quickly (seconds or less). Example: \([\text{Co(NH}_3)_6]^{2+}\)
  • Inert: react very slowly. Example: \([\text{Co(NH}_3)_6]^{3+}\), \([\text{Cr(NH}_3)_6]^{3+}\)

A complex can be thermodynamically stable but kinetically labile — or thermodynamically unstable but kinetically inert. These are independent properties!

Part B
What is the trans effect? Explain.

Definition

The trans effect is the ability of a ligand to increase the rate of substitution of the ligand in the trans position to itself, in square planar complexes (especially Pt²⁺).

Trans Effect Series

\[ \text{F}^- \approx \text{H}_2\text{O} < \text{OH}^- < \text{NH}_3 < \text{Cl}^- < \text{Br}^- < \text{I}^- < \text{NO}_2^- < \text{CN}^- \approx \text{CO} \approx \text{H}^- \]

Theories Explaining Trans Effect

  • Electrostatic (Polarisation) Theory: Trans ligands with high polarisability weaken the metal–trans bond by distorting electron density.
  • π-bonding Theory: Strong π-acceptors like CO and CN⁻ drain electron density from the metal in the trans direction, labilising the trans ligand.

Application — Synthesis of Pt Complexes

cis-[Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂] (cisplatin — anticancer drug) is made using the trans effect of Cl⁻ ligand to control where NH₃ substitutes.

d–d Transition Rules · Laporte Rules · Orgel Diagrams
2½ + 2 + 2 marks

(i) Selection Rules for d–d Transition

For an electron to undergo a d–d transition, it must obey:

  • Spin selection rule: \(\Delta S = 0\). Transitions between states of same spin multiplicity are allowed. Spin-forbidden transitions are weak.
  • Laporte selection rule: \(\Delta l = \pm 1\). d–d transitions are formally forbidden (both states have \(l = 2\)).

Despite being forbidden, d–d bands appear due to vibronic coupling (mixing with vibrations breaks symmetry).

(ii) Laporte Selection Rules

Laporte's rule states: transitions between orbitals of the same parity (g↔g or u↔u) are forbidden.

Since d-orbitals are gerade (g), d→d transitions are Laporte-forbidden.

  • Allowed: \(g \to u\) or \(u \to g\) (e.g., d→p transitions)
  • Forbidden: \(g \to g\) (d–d) or \(u \to u\)

Laporte-forbidden transitions still occur in complexes due to: vibronic coupling (non-centrosymmetric vibrations) and spin-orbit coupling. Their molar absorptivity \(\varepsilon\) is low (~1–100 L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹).

(iii) Orgel Diagrams

Orgel diagrams are energy-level diagrams that show how d-orbital terms split in a crystal field as ligand field strength increases. They are used to predict the number and type of d–d electronic transitions.

  • For \(d^1, d^6\) (octahedral) and \(d^4, d^9\) (tetrahedral): One spin-allowed transition — one absorption band expected.
  • For \(d^2, d^7\) (octahedral) and \(d^3, d^8\) (tetrahedral): Three spin-allowed transitions — up to three absorption bands possible.

Orgel diagrams are simplified (strong-field effects are not shown); Tanabe–Sugano diagrams are used for more complete analysis.

Organolithium Compounds — Preparation & Reactions
6½ marks

What are Organolithium Compounds?

Organolithium compounds (R–Li) contain a carbon–lithium bond. The C–Li bond is highly polar (almost ionic), making these extremely reactive and strong nucleophiles/bases.

Methods of Preparation

1. From alkyl halide + lithium metal:

\[ 2\text{Li} + \text{RX} \xrightarrow{\text{dry ether}} \text{RLi} + \text{LiX} \]

Example: \(2\text{Li} + \text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{Br} \to \text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{Li} + \text{LiBr}\)

2. Transmetalation (from R–Hg or R–Sn):

\[ \text{R}_2\text{Hg} + 2\text{Li} \to 2\text{RLi} + \text{Hg} \]

Reactions

(i) With CO₂:

\[ \text{RLi} + \text{CO}_2 \to \text{RCOOLi} \xrightarrow{\text{H}_3\text{O}^+} \text{RCOOH} \]

This gives a carboxylic acid — useful in synthesis.

(ii) With H₂O:

\[ \text{RLi} + \text{H}_2\text{O} \to \text{RH} + \text{LiOH} \]

R–Li is destroyed by water — these must be handled in dry, inert conditions.

(iii) With Bg₂ (Diborane):

\[ \text{RLi} + \text{B}_2\text{H}_6 \to \text{R}_3\text{B} + \text{LiBH}_4 \]

Gives trialkylborane — used in hydroboration reactions.

(iv) With C₆H₅CHO (Benzaldehyde):

\[ \text{RLi} + \text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{CHO} \to \text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{CH(OH)R} \]

Nucleophilic addition to the aldehyde group → gives a secondary alcohol.

Organometallic Compounds — Bonds & Classification by Ligand Nature
6½ marks

What are Organometallic Compounds?

Organometallic compounds contain at least one direct metal–carbon bond (M–C). They combine properties of both organic and inorganic chemistry.

Examples: \(\text{(C}_2\text{H}_5)_2\text{Zn}\) (diethylzinc), \(\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{Li}\) (phenyllithium), \(\text{Ferrocene Fe(C}_5\text{H}_5)_2\)

Types of Bonds in Organometallic Compounds

  • Ionic M–C bond: Highly electropositive metals (Na, K). Example: \(\text{C}_2\text{H}_5^-\text{Na}^+\)
  • Covalent M–C bond (σ-bond): Most common. Example: \(\text{Grignard reagent RMgX}\)
  • π-bond (backbonding): Metal donates electrons to π* orbital of ligand. Example: Metal carbonyls — \(\text{Ni(CO)}_4\)
  • Multicentre bonds: Bridging M–C–M bonds, as in \(\text{Al}_2(\text{CH}_3)_6\)

Phosphonitrilic Compounds — \((\text{PNCl}_2)_3\)
2 + 1½ + 1½ + 1½ marks

What are Phosphonitrilic Compounds?

Phosphonitrilic compounds (also called phosphazenes) have the general formula \((\text{PNX}_2)_n\) where X is typically Cl, F, or organic group. They have a backbone of alternating phosphorus and nitrogen atoms.

Example: \((\text{PNCl}_2)_3\) = trimeric phosphonitrilic chloride = phosphonitrilic chloride trimer

Preparation of \((\text{PNCl}_2)_3\)

Prepared by heating \(\text{PCl}_5\) and \(\text{NH}_4\text{Cl}\) in a chlorinated solvent (e.g., tetrachloroethane) at ~130°C:

\[ n\text{PCl}_5 + n\text{NH}_4\text{Cl} \xrightarrow{\Delta} (\text{PNCl}_2)_n + 4n\text{HCl} \]

The product is a mixture; the trimer \((\text{PNCl}_2)_3\) and tetramer \((\text{PNCl}_2)_4\) are isolated by distillation.

Properties of \((\text{PNCl}_2)_3\)

  • White crystalline solid, melting point ~114°C
  • Soluble in organic solvents like benzene, CCl₄
  • Thermally stable up to ~300°C
  • Reacts with water (hydrolysis) to give \(\text{H}_3\text{PO}_4\) and \(\text{NH}_3\)
  • Reacts with nucleophiles — Cl can be replaced by F, OR, NR₂ groups

Structure of \((\text{PNCl}_2)_3\)

The molecule is a 6-membered planar ring with alternating P and N atoms. Each P carries 2 Cl atoms. All P–N bonds are equal in length (~158 pm) — shorter than a single P–N bond — indicating delocalisation of electrons.

The P–N bond has partial double bond character due to back-donation of lone pair from N into empty d-orbitals of P → delocalized π-system over the ring (inorganic aromaticity).

Biological Importance of Ca²⁺ & Mg²⁺ · Oxygen Binding Curves
4 + 2½ marks
Part A
Explain biological importance of calcium and magnesium ions.

Biological Role of Calcium \((\text{Ca}^{2+})\)

  • Bone and teeth: Major component as hydroxyapatite \(\text{Ca}_{10}(\text{PO}_4)_6(\text{OH})_2\)
  • Muscle contraction: \(\text{Ca}^{2+}\) triggers release of neurotransmitters and activates troponin for muscle contraction
  • Blood clotting: Acts as cofactor in the coagulation cascade (factors IX and X)
  • Cell signalling: Acts as second messenger in hormone signalling pathways
  • Membrane permeability: Regulates ion channels in cell membranes
  • Enzyme activation: Activates enzymes like lipase and ATPase

Biological Role of Magnesium \((\text{Mg}^{2+})\)

  • Chlorophyll: Central atom in chlorophyll — essential for photosynthesis
  • ATP activation: \(\text{Mg}^{2+}\) binds to ATP to form Mg–ATP complex, required for almost all enzymatic reactions involving ATP
  • DNA/RNA stability: Stabilises the negative charge of phosphate backbone in nucleic acids
  • Enzyme cofactor: Activates over 300 enzymes including kinases, polymerases
  • Bone formation: About 60% of body Mg²⁺ is found in bones
  • Protein synthesis: Required for ribosome function
Part B
Draw the oxygen binding curve for Haemoglobin and Myoglobin.

Oxygen Binding Curves

Myoglobin (Mb) — single polypeptide chain, one haem unit → gives a hyperbolic (steep) curve. It has a very high affinity for O₂ (half-saturation at very low \(p_{\text{O}_2}\)).

Haemoglobin (Hb) — four subunits, shows cooperative binding → gives a characteristic sigmoidal (S-shaped) curve. Low initial affinity increases as more O₂ binds (positive cooperativity).

Key Difference: Mb (hyperbolic curve) always has higher O₂ affinity than Hb. At low pO₂ (tissues), Hb releases O₂ efficiently due to its sigmoidal behaviour — this is why Hb is ideal for O₂ transport.

The Bohr effect: Lower pH (higher CO₂ in tissues) shifts the Hb curve right → Hb releases more O₂ to active tissues.
