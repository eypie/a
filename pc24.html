UNIT-I

1. (a) Derive the de-Broglie wave equation and show that it proves the dual nature of matter. Describe the Davisson and Germer experiment for its verification. [5]

Derivation of the de-Broglie Wave Equation:

Louis de-Broglie proposed that just as light has a dual nature (wave and particle), matter also exhibits dual characteristics. To derive the equation, we combine the energy equations from Max Planck and Albert Einstein.

According to Planck's quantum theory, the energy of a photon is given by: $$E = h\nu = \frac{hc}{\lambda} \quad \text{--- (1)}$$ where \( h \) is Planck's constant, \( \nu \) is the frequency, \( c \) is the speed of light, and \( \lambda \) is the wavelength.

According to Einstein's mass-energy relationship, the energy of a particle with mass \( m \) is: $$E = mc^2 \quad \text{--- (2)}$$

Equating (1) and (2): $$mc^2 = \frac{hc}{\lambda} \implies \lambda = \frac{h}{mc}$$

For a material particle traveling with velocity \( v \) instead of the speed of light \( c \), we replace \( c \) with \( v \): $$\lambda = \frac{h}{mv} = \frac{h}{p}$$ where \( p = mv \) is the momentum of the particle. This is the de-Broglie equation. It proves the dual nature of matter because it relates a wave property (wavelength \( \lambda \)) directly to a particle property (momentum \( p \)).

Davisson and Germer Experiment:

This was the first experiment to experimentally prove the wave nature of electrons. In this setup, a beam of electrons emitted from a heated tungsten filament was accelerated using a potential difference and directed at a single nickel crystal.

The electrons scattered in different directions. A movable detector (Faraday cylinder) was used to measure the intensity of the scattered electron beam at various scattering angles (\( \theta \)).

Observation: A distinct peak in the intensity of the scattered beam was observed at an accelerating voltage of \( 50 \text{ V} \) and a scattering angle of \( 50^\circ \). This peak is caused by the constructive interference of electron waves scattered from the atomic layers of the nickel crystal. Applying Bragg's law of diffraction (\( n\lambda = 2d \sin\theta \)), the calculated wavelength matched exactly with the theoretical wavelength predicted by de-Broglie's equation, thereby confirming the wave nature of electrons.

1. (b) If an electron is accelerated by a potential difference of 100 volts, calculate the wavelength of an electron. [2]

Solution:

The kinetic energy (\( E_k \)) gained by an electron accelerated through a potential difference \( V \) is given by: $$E_k = eV$$

The de-Broglie wavelength is related to kinetic energy by: $$\lambda = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2mE_k}} = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2meV}}$$

For an electron, substituting the standard values (\( h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34} \text{ J s} \), \( m = 9.1 \times 10^{-31} \text{ kg} \), \( e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \text{ C} \)), we get a simplified formula: $$\lambda = \sqrt{\frac{150}{V}} \text{ \AA} \approx \frac{12.27}{\sqrt{V}} \text{ \AA}$$

Given \( V = 100 \text{ volts} \): $$\lambda = \frac{12.27}{\sqrt{100}} = \frac{12.27}{10} = 1.227 \text{ \AA}$$

Answer: The wavelength of the electron is \( 1.227 \text{ \AA} \) (or \( 0.1227 \text{ nm} \)).

2. Write short notes on the following:
(a) Compton's effect
(b) Plank's quantum theory
(c) Heisenberg's uncertainty principle [2+2+3=7]

(a) Compton's Effect:

Discovered by Arthur Compton, this effect refers to the increase in the wavelength of X-rays or gamma rays when they are scattered by electrons in a target material (like graphite). The incident photon collides elastically with an electron, transferring some of its energy and momentum. Consequently, the scattered photon has lower energy and a longer wavelength. The shift in wavelength (\( \Delta\lambda \)) is given by: $$\Delta\lambda = \lambda' - \lambda = \frac{h}{m_0 c}(1 - \cos\theta)$$ where \( m_0 \) is the rest mass of the electron and \( \theta \) is the scattering angle. This effect provides strong evidence for the particle (photon) nature of light.

(b) Planck's Quantum Theory:

Proposed by Max Planck in 1900, this theory states that atoms and molecules can emit or absorb energy only in discrete, discontinuous quantities, rather than continuously. The smallest packet of energy is called a "quantum" (or "photon" in the case of light). The energy \( E \) of each quantum is directly proportional to the frequency \( \nu \) of the radiation: $$E = h\nu$$ where \( h \) is Planck's constant (\( 6.626 \times 10^{-34} \text{ J s} \)).

(c) Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle:

Formulated by Werner Heisenberg, this principle states that it is impossible to simultaneously determine the exact position and the exact momentum (or velocity) of a microscopic particle, such as an electron, with absolute certainty. Mathematically, the product of the uncertainties in position (\( \Delta x \)) and momentum (\( \Delta p \)) must be greater than or equal to \( \frac{h}{4\pi} \): $$\Delta x \cdot \Delta p \ge \frac{h}{4\pi}$$ This principle implies that the concept of definite atomic orbits (as proposed by Bohr) is invalid, leading to the probabilistic approach of quantum mechanics.

3. Explain the Hybridization. Calculate mixing coefficient of SP, \(SP^2\) and \(SP^3\) hybrid orbitals and find wave function of these. [1+2+2+2=7]

Hybridization:

Hybridization is the quantum mechanical process of mixing atomic orbitals of slightly different energies (like \(s\) and \(p\) orbitals) in an atom to form a new set of equivalent orbitals known as hybrid orbitals. These hybrid orbitals have identical shapes and energies and are arranged in specific geometric geometries to minimize repulsion, resulting in stronger and more directional chemical bonds.

1. \(sp\) Hybridization:

Involves mixing one \(s\) and one \(p\) (e.g., \(p_x\)) orbital. It forms two equivalent \(sp\) hybrid orbitals.
Let the wave functions be: $$\psi_1 = a_1\phi_s + b_1\phi_{p_x}$$ $$\psi_2 = a_2\phi_s + b_2\phi_{p_x}$$ Since the \(s\) character is distributed equally, \(a_1^2 = a_2^2 = \frac{1}{2}\) \(\implies a_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}, a_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\).
For normalization, \(a_1^2 + b_1^2 = 1 \implies b_1^2 = 1 - \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{2} \implies b_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\).
For orthogonality, \(a_1a_2 + b_1b_2 = 0 \implies \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right) + \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)b_2 = 0 \implies b_2 = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\).
Wave functions: $$\psi_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\phi_s + \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\phi_{p_x}$$ $$\psi_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\phi_s - \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\phi_{p_x}$$

2. \(sp^2\) Hybridization:

Involves one \(s\) and two \(p\) orbitals (\(p_x, p_y\)). It forms three equivalent orbitals. Total \(s\) character is \(1/3\) per orbital.
Wave functions:
$$\psi_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\phi_s + \sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}\phi_{p_x}$$ $$\psi_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\phi_s - \frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}\phi_{p_x} + \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\phi_{p_y}$$ $$\psi_3 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\phi_s - \frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}\phi_{p_x} - \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\phi_{p_y}$$

3. \(sp^3\) Hybridization:

Involves one \(s\) and three \(p\) orbitals. Forms four equivalent hybrid orbitals. Total \(s\) character is \(1/4\).
Wave functions:
$$\psi_1 = \frac{1}{2}(\phi_s + \phi_{p_x} + \phi_{p_y} + \phi_{p_z})$$ $$\psi_2 = \frac{1}{2}(\phi_s + \phi_{p_x} - \phi_{p_y} - \phi_{p_z})$$ $$\psi_3 = \frac{1}{2}(\phi_s - \phi_{p_x} + \phi_{p_y} - \phi_{p_z})$$ $$\psi_4 = \frac{1}{2}(\phi_s - \phi_{p_x} - \phi_{p_y} + \phi_{p_z})$$

4. Derive the expression for wave function and the associated energy for the molecular orbitals of \(H_2^+\) ion by LCAO method. [7]

Linear Combination of Atomic Orbitals (LCAO) for \(H_2^+\):

The \(H_2^+\) ion consists of two protons (A and B) and one electron. The molecular orbital wave function (\(\psi\)) is approximated by combining the atomic orbital wave functions of the two hydrogen atoms (\(\phi_A\) and \(\phi_B\)): $$\psi = c_1\phi_A + c_2\phi_B$$

Energy Expression:
Using the variation theorem, the energy \(E\) of the molecular orbital is: $$E = \frac{\int \psi \hat{H} \psi d\tau}{\int \psi^2 d\tau}$$ Substituting \(\psi = c_1\phi_A + c_2\phi_B\): $$E = \frac{c_1^2 H_{AA} + c_2^2 H_{BB} + 2c_1c_2 H_{AB}}{c_1^2 + c_2^2 + 2c_1c_2 S_{AB}}$$ where \(H_{AA} = \int \phi_A \hat{H} \phi_A d\tau = H_{BB}\) (Coulomb integral), \(H_{AB} = \int \phi_A \hat{H} \phi_B d\tau\) (Resonance/Exchange integral), and \(S_{AB} = \int \phi_A \phi_B d\tau\) (Overlap integral).

Minimizing energy with respect to \(c_1\) and \(c_2\) (\(\frac{\partial E}{\partial c_1} = 0, \frac{\partial E}{\partial c_2} = 0\)) yields the secular equations. Solving the secular determinant gives two energy levels: $$E_+ = \frac{H_{AA} + H_{AB}}{1 + S_{AB}} \quad \text{(Bonding Orbital Energy)}$$ $$E_- = \frac{H_{AA} - H_{AB}}{1 - S_{AB}} \quad \text{(Antibonding Orbital Energy)}$$

Wave Functions:
For \(E_+\), \(c_1 = c_2\). Normalizing yields: $$\psi_+ = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2(1 + S_{AB})}} (\phi_A + \phi_B) \quad \text{(Bonding MO, } \sigma_{1s} \text{)}$$ For \(E_-\), \(c_1 = -c_2\). Normalizing yields: $$\psi_- = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2(1 - S_{AB})}} (\phi_A - \phi_B) \quad \text{(Antibonding MO, } \sigma^*_{1s} \text{)}$$

5. Write short notes on the following:
(a) Frank-Condon principle
(b) Degrees of freedom
(c) Stokes and anti-stokes lines [3+2+2=7]

(a) Franck-Condon Principle:

This principle states that electronic transitions occur so rapidly (in about \(10^{-15}\) seconds) compared to the motion of nuclei that the internuclear distance does not change significantly during the transition. As a result, electronic transitions are represented as vertical lines on a potential energy diagram. The most probable transition is the one that involves no change in the nuclear geometry, meaning the vibrational wave functions of the initial and final states overlap to a maximum extent.

(b) Degrees of Freedom:

The total number of independent coordinates required to completely specify the position and orientation of a molecule in space is called its degrees of freedom. For a molecule with \(N\) atoms, the total degrees of freedom is \(3N\). These are divided into:

  • Translational: Always 3 (motion along x, y, and z axes).
  • Rotational: 2 for linear molecules, 3 for non-linear molecules.
  • Vibrational: \(3N - 5\) for linear molecules, and \(3N - 6\) for non-linear molecules.

(c) Stokes and Anti-Stokes Lines:

In Raman spectroscopy, when monochromatic light is scattered by a sample, most photons scatter elastically (Rayleigh scattering). However, a small fraction scatters inelastically (Raman scattering).

  • Stokes Lines: Lines found at lower frequencies (longer wavelengths) than the incident light. This happens when the molecule absorbs part of the photon's energy and jumps to a higher vibrational state.
  • Anti-Stokes Lines: Lines found at higher frequencies (shorter wavelengths) than the incident light. This occurs when an already excited molecule gives up its energy to the photon, returning to a lower energy state.

6. Discuss the rotational spectra of a diatomic rigid rotor. Derive expression for the frequency of lines of rotational spectra. [4+3=7]

Rotational Spectra of a Diatomic Rigid Rotor:

A diatomic molecule can be modeled as a rigid rotor, consisting of two point masses (\(m_1\) and \(m_2\)) connected by a rigid, weightless bond of fixed length \(r\). The molecule rotates about an axis passing through its center of mass.

The moment of inertia \(I\) of the molecule is given by: $$I = \mu r^2$$ where \( \mu = \frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2} \) is the reduced mass.

Derivation of Frequency Expression:

According to quantum mechanics, the allowed rotational energy levels are quantized and given by the solution to the Schrödinger equation: $$E_J = \frac{h^2}{8\pi^2 I} J(J + 1) \text{ Joules}$$ where \( J \) is the rotational quantum number (\( J = 0, 1, 2, \dots \)).

In spectroscopy, energy is often expressed in terms of wavenumbers (\(\bar{\nu}\)), using the relation \( E = hc\bar{\nu} \): $$\varepsilon_J = \frac{E_J}{hc} = \frac{h}{8\pi^2 I c} J(J + 1) \text{ cm}^{-1}$$ Let \( B = \frac{h}{8\pi^2 I c} \) be the rotational constant. Thus: $$\varepsilon_J = B J(J + 1)$$

The selection rule for a rotational transition is \(\Delta J = \pm 1\). In absorption, the transition is from a lower state \(J\) to an upper state \(J+1\). The energy difference (\(\Delta\varepsilon\)), which corresponds to the wavenumber of the absorbed spectral line, is: $$\bar{\nu} = \varepsilon_{J+1} - \varepsilon_J$$ $$\bar{\nu} = B(J+1)(J+1+1) - B J(J+1)$$ $$\bar{\nu} = B[(J+1)(J+2) - J(J+1)] = B[J^2 + 3J + 2 - J^2 - J]$$ $$\bar{\nu} = 2B(J + 1) \text{ cm}^{-1}$$

For \(J = 0\), \(\bar{\nu} = 2B\)
For \(J = 1\), \(\bar{\nu} = 4B\)
For \(J = 2\), \(\bar{\nu} = 6B\)

Thus, the rotational spectrum consists of a series of equally spaced lines separated by \(2B\).

7. Write short notes on the following:
(a) Quantum yield
(b) Photosensitized reactions
(c) Radiative and non-radiative processes [2+3+2=7]

(a) Quantum Yield (\(\Phi\)):

Also known as quantum efficiency, it is a measure of the efficiency of a photochemical reaction. It is defined as the ratio of the number of molecules reacting (or formed) in a given time to the number of photons absorbed by the system in the same time. $$\Phi = \frac{\text{Number of molecules undergoing reaction}}{\text{Number of quanta (photons) absorbed}}$$ According to the Stark-Einstein law of photochemical equivalence, primary processes should have \(\Phi = 1\). However, secondary reactions can cause the overall yield to be very high (\(10^4\) to \(10^6\), as in chain reactions) or very low (due to deactivation).

(b) Photosensitized Reactions:

In some photochemical reactions, the reactant molecules themselves do not absorb the incident light. A foreign substance called a "photosensitizer" is added, which absorbs the light, gets excited, and then transfers its excitation energy to the reactant molecules, causing them to undergo a chemical reaction. The sensitizer itself returns to its ground state unchanged.
Example: Dissociation of \(H_2\) using Mercury vapor as a sensitizer. Mercury absorbs UV light (253.7 nm) to form excited \(Hg^*\), which collides with \(H_2\) molecules, transferring energy to dissociate them into H atoms.

(c) Radiative and Non-radiative Processes:

These processes describe how an electronically excited molecule gets rid of its excess energy (visualized in a Jablonski Diagram).

  • Radiative Processes: Involve the emission of a photon. Examples include:
    • Fluorescence: Transition from the first excited singlet state to the ground singlet state (\(S_1 \to S_0\)). It is fast and stops when the light source is removed.
    • Phosphorescence: Transition from a triplet state to a ground singlet state (\(T_1 \to S_0\)). It involves a change in spin multiplicity (forbidden transition), making it a slow, delayed emission.
  • Non-radiative Processes: The excess energy is dissipated as heat without emitting light. Examples include:
    • Internal Conversion (IC): Transition between states of the same spin multiplicity (e.g., \(S_2 \to S_1\)).
    • Intersystem Crossing (ISC): Transition between states of different spin multiplicity (e.g., \(S_1 \to T_1\)).

8. What is meant by molar polarization? Derive Clausius-Mossotti equation. [2+5=7]

Molar Polarization (\(P_m\)):

Molar polarization is a measure of the total polarizability of a mole of a substance when placed in an electric field. It connects the macroscopic dielectric constant of the material to the microscopic polarizability of its molecules. It is defined as: $$P_m = \frac{4}{3}\pi N_A \alpha$$ where \(N_A\) is Avogadro's number and \(\alpha\) is the polarizability of a single molecule.

Derivation of the Clausius-Mossotti Equation:

When a dielectric material is placed in an external electric field \(E\), the internal field actually experienced by a single molecule inside the dielectric is greater than the applied field. This is called the local field (\(E_{loc}\)). For an isotropic dielectric, Lorentz showed that the local field is given by: $$E_{loc} = E + \frac{P}{3\varepsilon_0}$$ where \(P\) is the macroscopic polarization vector (dipole moment per unit volume) and \(\varepsilon_0\) is the permittivity of free space.

The macroscopic polarization \(P\) is also related to the microscopic polarizability \(\alpha\) and the number of molecules per unit volume \(N\): $$P = N \alpha E_{loc} = N \alpha \left( E + \frac{P}{3\varepsilon_0} \right) \quad \text{--- (1)}$$

From electromagnetic theory, the dielectric constant (relative permittivity) \(\varepsilon_r\) is related to macroscopic polarization \(P\) and the applied field \(E\) by: $$P = \varepsilon_0 (\varepsilon_r - 1) E \implies E = \frac{P}{\varepsilon_0(\varepsilon_r - 1)} \quad \text{--- (2)}$$

Substitute (2) into (1): $$P = N \alpha \left( \frac{P}{\varepsilon_0(\varepsilon_r - 1)} + \frac{P}{3\varepsilon_0} \right)$$ Dividing both sides by \(P\): $$1 = \frac{N \alpha}{\varepsilon_0} \left( \frac{1}{\varepsilon_r - 1} + \frac{1}{3} \right)$$ $$\frac{\varepsilon_0}{N \alpha} = \frac{3 + (\varepsilon_r - 1)}{3(\varepsilon_r - 1)} = \frac{\varepsilon_r + 2}{3(\varepsilon_r - 1)}$$ Rearranging to solve for polarizability: $$\frac{\varepsilon_r - 1}{\varepsilon_r + 2} = \frac{N \alpha}{3\varepsilon_0}$$ This is the general Clausius-Mossotti relation. For a molar volume, \(N = \frac{N_A \rho}{M}\), where \(M\) is the molar mass and \(\rho\) is the density. Substituting this gives the Clausius-Mossotti Equation for molar polarization (\(P_m\)): $$P_m = \left( \frac{\varepsilon_r - 1}{\varepsilon_r + 2} \right) \frac{M}{\rho} = \frac{N_A \alpha}{3\varepsilon_0}$$ (Note: In CGS units, the \(4\pi\) factor replaces \(\varepsilon_0\), yielding \( \frac{\varepsilon_r - 1}{\varepsilon_r + 2} \frac{M}{\rho} = \frac{4}{3}\pi N_A \alpha \)).

9. (a) Prove that the relative lowering of vapour pressure of solution is directly proportional to osmotic pressure of solution. [4]

Proof:

Let the vapour pressure of a pure solvent be \(P_0\) and that of the solution containing a non-volatile solute be \(P\). According to Raoult's Law, the relative lowering of vapour pressure (RLVP) is equal to the mole fraction of the solute (\(x_B\)): $$\frac{P_0 - P}{P_0} = x_B \quad \text{--- (1)}$$

For a very dilute solution, \(n_B \ll n_A\) (where \(n_A\) and \(n_B\) are moles of solvent and solute, respectively). Thus, the mole fraction becomes: $$x_B = \frac{n_B}{n_A + n_B} \approx \frac{n_B}{n_A} = \frac{n_B}{W_A / M_A} = \frac{n_B \cdot M_A}{W_A}$$ where \(W_A\) is the mass of the solvent and \(M_A\) is the molar mass of the solvent.

Substituting into (1): $$\frac{P_0 - P}{P_0} = \frac{n_B M_A}{W_A} \quad \text{--- (2)}$$

Now, according to the Van't Hoff equation for osmotic pressure (\(\pi\)): $$\pi V = n_B R T \implies n_B = \frac{\pi V}{R T} \quad \text{--- (3)}$$ where \(V\) is the volume of the solution.

Substituting (3) into (2): $$\frac{P_0 - P}{P_0} = \frac{\pi V M_A}{W_A R T}$$

Since the solution is very dilute, the volume of the solution \(V\) is approximately equal to the volume of the solvent. Density of the solvent \(\rho = \frac{W_A}{V} \implies \frac{V}{W_A} = \frac{1}{\rho}\). Substituting this: $$\frac{P_0 - P}{P_0} = \pi \left( \frac{M_A}{\rho R T} \right)$$

At a constant temperature, for a given solvent, \(M_A\), \(\rho\), \(R\), and \(T\) are all constants. Let the constant be \(K = \frac{M_A}{\rho R T}\). Therefore: $$\frac{P_0 - P}{P_0} = K \cdot \pi \implies \frac{P_0 - P}{P_0} \propto \pi$$ This proves that the relative lowering of vapour pressure is directly proportional to the osmotic pressure of the dilute solution.

9. (b) Calculate the osmotic pressure of \(\frac{M}{20}\) solution of non-volatile solute at 27°C. (\(R=0.0821 \text{ L atm K}^{-1} \text{mol}^{-1}\)) [2]

Solution:

We use the Van't Hoff equation for osmotic pressure: $$\pi = CRT$$

Given parameters:

  • Concentration \(C = \frac{M}{20} = \frac{1}{20} \text{ mol/L} = 0.05 \text{ M}\)
  • Temperature \(T = 27^\circ\text{C} = 27 + 273 = 300 \text{ K}\)
  • Gas constant \(R = 0.0821 \text{ L atm K}^{-1} \text{mol}^{-1}\)

Calculation: $$\pi = 0.05 \times 0.0821 \times 300$$ $$\pi = 0.05 \times 24.63$$ $$\pi = 1.2315 \text{ atm}$$

Answer: The osmotic pressure of the solution is \( 1.2315 \text{ atm} \).

10. Write short notes on the following:
(a) Vant Hoff's factor
(b) Colligative properties of dilute solutions
(c) Activity and activity coefficient [2+2+2=6]

(a) Van't Hoff's Factor (\(i\)):

When an electrolyte is dissolved in a solvent, it may dissociate or associate, causing the number of particles in the solution to change. The Van't Hoff factor (\(i\)) is introduced to account for this deviation in colligative properties. It is defined as: $$i = \frac{\text{Observed colligative property}}{\text{Theoretical colligative property}} = \frac{\text{Actual number of particles after dissociation/association}}{\text{Initial number of molecules dissolved}}$$ For dissociation (e.g., NaCl), \(i > 1\). For association (e.g., acetic acid in benzene), \(i < 1\). For non-electrolytes (e.g., glucose), \(i = 1\).

(b) Colligative Properties of Dilute Solutions:

Colligative properties are properties of a dilute solution that depend exclusively on the number of solute particles (molecules or ions) present in a given volume of solvent, and not on the chemical identity or nature of the solute. The four main colligative properties are:

  1. Relative lowering of vapour pressure (\(\frac{\Delta P}{P_0}\))
  2. Elevation in boiling point (\(\Delta T_b\))
  3. Depression in freezing point (\(\Delta T_f\))
  4. Osmotic pressure (\(\pi\))
These properties are extremely useful in determining the molar masses of unknown non-volatile solutes.

(c) Activity and Activity Coefficient:

In concentrated or non-ideal solutions, solute particles interact with each other, meaning the solution does not behave ideally according to Raoult's or Henry's Law. To apply thermodynamic equations (derived for ideal solutions) to real solutions, G.N. Lewis introduced the concept of activity (\(a\)). Activity represents the "effective concentration" of a species in a mixture.

The ratio of activity to the actual concentration (\(c\)) is called the activity coefficient (\(\gamma\)): $$a = \gamma c$$ In very dilute (ideal) solutions, particle interactions are negligible, so \(\gamma \approx 1\) and \(a \approx c\). As the concentration increases, the interactions increase, and the activity coefficient deviates from 1.
