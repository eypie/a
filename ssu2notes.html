Unit-II: Band Theory of Solids and Semiconductors

How electrons behave in solid materials — from discrete energy levels to continuous energy bands.

1. Band Theory of Solids

Formation of Bands

When atoms are far apart, they have discrete energy levels. As they come closer to form a solid, their wave functions overlap. Due to the Pauli Exclusion Principle, no two electrons can have the same set of quantum numbers. Consequently, each discrete energy level splits into a large number of closely spaced levels, forming a continuous Energy Band.

📊 [Diagram: Discrete atomic levels splitting into energy bands as interatomic distance decreases]

Periodic Potential and Bloch Theorem

In a crystal, the nuclei are arranged in a regular, repeating lattice. An electron moves in a periodic potential \(V(x) = V(x+a)\), where \(a\) is the lattice constant.

  • Bloch Theorem: It states that the wave function \(\psi(x)\) for an electron in a periodic potential is a plane wave modulated by a periodic function \(u_k(x)\).
  • Formula:
    $$\psi_k(x) = u_k(x) e^{ikx}$$
    Where \(u_k(x) = u_k(x+a)\) is periodic and \(k\) is the wave vector.

Kronig-Penney Model

This is a mathematical model that simplifies the periodic potential into a series of rectangular potential wells. It explains the existence of Energy Gaps (Forbidden Bands).

  • Key Result: The allowed values of energy are determined by the condition:
    $$P \frac{\sin \alpha a}{\alpha a} + \cos \alpha a = \cos ka$$
    Where \(P\) is the "scattering power" of the potential barrier. If the equation is not satisfied, that energy region is a Forbidden Band.
📊 [Diagram: Kronig-Penney periodic potential wells and resulting E-k dispersion]

Brillouin Zones

Brillouin zones are boundaries in \(k\)-space (momentum space) where the electron undergoes Bragg reflection.

  • First Brillouin Zone: The region between \(k = -\frac{\pi}{a}\) and \(k = +\frac{\pi}{a}\).
  • Physical significance: At these boundaries, energy gaps appear.

Crystal Momentum and Effective Mass

  • Crystal Momentum: Defined as \(p = \hbar k\). It is not the same as classical momentum because the electron interacts with the lattice.
  • Effective Mass (\(m^*\)): In a crystal, an electron moves as if it has a different mass than its rest mass due to internal forces from the lattice.
  • Formula:
    $$m^* = \frac{\hbar^2}{\frac{d^2E}{dk^2}}$$
  • Physical Origin: It depends on the curvature of the \(E-k\) (Energy-momentum) diagram.
    • Large curvature → Small effective mass.
    • Small curvature → Large effective mass.

Negative Effective Mass and Holes

Near the top of an energy band, the curvature \(\frac{d^2E}{dk^2}\) becomes negative, meaning the effective mass is negative.

  • Holes: A vacant state in the valence band (missing electron) behaves like a particle with a positive charge and positive mass.

Energy Dispersion Relations

This relates the energy \(E\) of an electron to its wave vector \(k\).

  1. Weak Binding (Nearly Free Electron Model): Assumes electrons are almost free, and the periodic potential is a small perturbation. It explains energy gaps at zone boundaries.
  2. Tight Binding Model: Assumes electrons are tightly bound to their parent atoms. Energy bands arise from the slight overlapping of atomic wave functions.

2. Semiconductors

Band Structure Comparison

  • Conductors: The valence and conduction bands overlap. There is no energy gap (\(E_g = 0\)).
  • Insulators: Large energy gap (\(E_g > 3\text{ eV}\)). Electrons cannot jump to the conduction band.
  • Semiconductors: Small energy gap (\(E_g \approx 1\text{ eV}\)). Thermal energy can excite electrons at room temperature.
📊 [Diagram: Band structure comparison - Conductor (overlap), Semiconductor (small gap), Insulator (large gap)]

Direct and Indirect Band Gaps

  • Direct Band Gap: The maximum of the valence band and minimum of the conduction band occur at the same \(k\) value. (e.g., GaAs). Useful for LEDs and Lasers.
  • Indirect Band Gap: The maximum and minimum occur at different \(k\) values. (e.g., Silicon, Germanium). Energy is lost as heat (phonons).
📊 [Diagram: Direct vs Indirect band gap showing E vs k curves]

Generation and Recombination

  • Generation: Process where an electron-hole pair is created (usually by absorbing light or heat).
  • Recombination: Process where an electron from the conduction band falls into a hole in the valence band, releasing energy.

Mobility (\(\mu\))

It is the ease with which charge carriers move through a conductor/semiconductor when an electric field is applied.

  • Formula:
    $$\mu = \frac{v_d}{E}$$
    Where \(v_d\) is drift velocity and \(E\) is the electric field. Unit: \(m^2/V \cdot s\).

3. Hall Effect in Semiconductors

If a magnetic field (\(B\)) is applied perpendicular to the direction of current (\(I\)) in a sample, a voltage (\(V_H\)) is developed perpendicular to both \(I\) and \(B\). This is the Hall Effect.

📊 [Diagram: Hall Effect setup showing current I, magnetic field B, and Hall voltage V_H]

Key Definitions and Formulas

  1. Hall Voltage (\(V_H\)):
    $$V_H = \frac{BI}{\rho w}$$
    (where \(\rho\) is charge density and \(w\) is width).
  2. Hall Coefficient (\(R_H\)): It indicates the type of charge carriers.
    $$R_H = \frac{1}{ne} \text{ (for electrons)} \quad \text{or} \quad R_H = \frac{1}{pe} \text{ (for holes)}$$
    • Negative \(R_H\) → n-type semiconductor.
    • Positive \(R_H\) → p-type semiconductor.
  3. Carrier Concentration (\(n\) or \(p\)):
    $$n = \frac{1}{R_H e}$$
  4. Mobility (\(\mu\)):
    $$\mu = \sigma |R_H|$$
    Where \(\sigma\) is conductivity.
  5. Hall Angle (\(\theta_H\)): The angle between the net electric field and the applied electric field.
    $$\tan \theta_H = \frac{E_y}{E_x} = \mu B$$

Applications of Hall Effect:

  • Determining the sign of charge carriers (n-type or p-type).
  • Measuring carrier concentration (\(n\)).
  • Measuring mobility (\(\mu\)).
  • Measuring magnetic flux density.
