1. (a) Derive de-Broglie's Equation. How does this equation explain the dual nature of wave? [2½+1½][cite: 4]

Derivation of the de-Broglie Equation:

Louis de-Broglie proposed that matter, like light, exhibits both wave and particle properties. The equation is derived by equating the energy equations of a photon from Planck's and Einstein's theories.

From Planck's quantum theory, the energy ($E$) of a photon is: $$E = h\nu = \frac{hc}{\lambda} \quad \text{--- (1)}$$ where $h$ is Planck's constant, $\nu$ is the frequency, $c$ is the speed of light, and $\lambda$ is the wavelength.

From Einstein's mass-energy equivalence, the energy of a particle with mass $m$ is: $$E = mc^2 \quad \text{--- (2)}$$

Equating (1) and (2): $$mc^2 = \frac{hc}{\lambda} \implies \lambda = \frac{h}{mc}$$

For a material particle moving with velocity $v$ (instead of the speed of light $c$), we replace $c$ with $v$: $$\lambda = \frac{h}{mv} = \frac{h}{p}$$ where $p = mv$ is the momentum of the particle. This is the de-Broglie equation.

Explanation of Dual Nature:

The de-Broglie equation specifically equates a wave characteristic (wavelength, $\lambda$) on one side with a particle characteristic (momentum, $p$) on the other side. This establishes a direct mathematical bridge between the wave nature and the particle nature of matter, proving that moving particles possess an associated wave.

1. (b) Calculate the velocity and energy of an electron if the de-Broglie wavelength of the wave associate with it is $10^{-10}m$. [2½][cite: 4]

Given:

  • Wavelength ($\lambda$) = $10^{-10} \text{ m}$
  • Mass of electron ($m$) = $9.11 \times 10^{-31} \text{ kg}$
  • Planck's constant ($h$) = $6.626 \times 10^{-34} \text{ J}\cdot\text{s}$

1. Calculating Velocity ($v$):

Using the de-Broglie equation $\lambda = \frac{h}{mv}$, we rearrange for $v$: $$v = \frac{h}{m\lambda}$$ $$v = \frac{6.626 \times 10^{-34}}{9.11 \times 10^{-31} \times 10^{-10}}$$ $$v = \frac{6.626 \times 10^{-34}}{9.11 \times 10^{-41}} = 0.7273 \times 10^7 \text{ m/s} = 7.273 \times 10^6 \text{ m/s}$$

2. Calculating Energy ($E$):

Kinetic energy is given by $E = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$: $$E = \frac{1}{2} \times 9.11 \times 10^{-31} \times (7.273 \times 10^6)^2$$ $$E = 0.5 \times 9.11 \times 10^{-31} \times 52.89 \times 10^{12}$$ $$E = 2.41 \times 10^{-17} \text{ Joules}$$

2. Write short notes on the following:-
(i) The Heisenberg's uncertainty principle. [2½]
(ii) Hamiltonian operator. [2]
(iii) Postulates of Quantum Mechanics. [2½][cite: 4]

(i) Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle:

This fundamental principle states that it is impossible to simultaneously determine both the exact position and the exact momentum of a microscopic particle (like an electron) with absolute accuracy. If one is measured precisely, the other becomes increasingly uncertain. Mathematically, the product of the uncertainties is always greater than or equal to a constant: $$\Delta x \cdot \Delta p \ge \frac{h}{4\pi}$$ where $\Delta x$ is the uncertainty in position and $\Delta p$ is the uncertainty in momentum.

(ii) Hamiltonian Operator ($\hat{H}$):

In quantum mechanics, the Hamiltonian operator is the total energy operator of a system. It represents the sum of the kinetic energy ($\hat{T}$) and potential energy ($\hat{V}$) operators of the particles in the system. When the Hamiltonian operator is applied to the wave function of a system, it yields the total energy ($E$) of that state, forming the basis of the Schrödinger equation ($\hat{H}\psi = E\psi$). $$\hat{H} = -\frac{h^2}{8\pi^2 m}\nabla^2 + V$$

(iii) Postulates of Quantum Mechanics:

  • State Function: The physical state of a quantum system is completely described by a wave function $\psi$.
  • Operators: For every physically measurable property (observable) like momentum or energy, there exists a corresponding linear, Hermitian operator.
  • Eigenvalues: The only possible values obtained when measuring an observable are the eigenvalues of its corresponding operator ($\hat{A}\psi = a\psi$).
  • Expectation Value: For a system in state $\psi$, the average or expectation value of an observable $\hat{A}$ is given by $\frac{\int \psi^* \hat{A} \psi d\tau}{\int \psi^* \psi d\tau}$.

3. Taking the example of hydrogen molecule, explain molecular orbital theory. Compare this theory and discuss which theory is superior over the other. [3+2+2][cite: 4]

Molecular Orbital Theory (MOT) using Hydrogen ($H_2$):

MOT states that atomic orbitals (AOs) combine to form molecular orbitals (MOs) that span the entire molecule. When two Hydrogen atoms approach each other, their $1s$ atomic orbitals undergo linear combination (LCAO):

  • Addition (Constructive Interference): Forms a bonding molecular orbital ($\sigma_{1s}$) with lower energy. Electron density is concentrated between the nuclei, pulling them together.
  • Subtraction (Destructive Interference): Forms an antibonding molecular orbital ($\sigma^*_{1s}$) with higher energy. A node exists between the nuclei, pushing them apart.

In the $H_2$ molecule, the two available electrons completely fill the lower-energy $\sigma_{1s}$ bonding orbital with opposite spins. The bond order is calculated as $\frac{N_b - N_a}{2} = \frac{2 - 0}{2} = 1$, indicating a stable single bond.

Comparison of MOT with Valence Bond Theory (VBT):

Valence Bond Theory (VBT) Molecular Orbital Theory (MOT)
Atoms retain their individual identity in a molecule. Atoms lose their individual identity; electrons belong to the whole molecule.
Bonds are formed by overlapping of half-filled AOs. MOs are formed by LCAO of proper symmetry and energy.
Fails to explain the paramagnetic nature of $O_2$. Successfully explains the paramagnetic nature of $O_2$.

Superiority of MOT:

Molecular Orbital Theory is generally considered superior. While VBT is visually intuitive and works well for simple geometries, MOT accurately explains complex phenomena that VBT fails to address. MOT naturally predicts the paramagnetic behavior of the oxygen molecule, the existence of odd-electron species (like $NO$), and explains fractional bond orders (like in $H_2^+$).

4. Write note on the following:-
(i) Hybrid orbital
(ii) Bonding and antibonding orbitals
(iii) Dissimilarities of MOT&VBT [7][cite: 4]

(i) Hybrid Orbital:

Hybrid orbitals are formed through hybridization—the quantum mechanical mixing of pure atomic orbitals of an atom (like $s$ and $p$) that have slightly different energies. This mixing produces a new set of equivalent orbitals with identical shapes and energies. Hybrid orbitals are more directional than pure orbitals, resulting in stronger bond formation and specific molecular geometries (e.g., $sp^3$ forms tetrahedral geometry).

(ii) Bonding and Antibonding Orbitals:

  • Bonding Molecular Orbital (BMO): Formed by the in-phase addition of atomic wave functions ($\psi = \phi_A + \phi_B$). It has lower energy than the parent atomic orbitals. The electron density is heavily concentrated in the internuclear region, neutralizing nuclear repulsion and stabilizing the bond.
  • Antibonding Molecular Orbital (ABMO): Formed by the out-of-phase subtraction of atomic wave functions ($\psi = \phi_A - \phi_B$). It has higher energy than the parent atomic orbitals. There is a nodal plane between the nuclei where electron density is zero, leading to nuclear repulsion and destabilization.

(iii) Dissimilarities of MOT & VBT:

(See the comparison table in Question 3). The core dissimilarity lies in electron localization: VBT views bonding electrons as localized strictly between the two specific atoms sharing them, whereas MOT views electrons as completely delocalized over the entire molecular framework.

5. Explain the following in details:-
(i) Isotope effect
(ii) Pure vibrational spectrum
(iii) Selection rule for electronic transitions [7][cite: 4]

(i) Isotope Effect:

When an atom in a molecule is replaced by its heavier isotope, the chemical properties remain largely unchanged, but the physical properties dependent on mass (reduced mass, $\mu$) are altered. In spectroscopy, the rotational constant $B$ is inversely proportional to reduced mass. Therefore, substituting a heavier isotope increases $\mu$, decreases $B$, and causes the rotational spectral lines to shift closer together (towards lower frequencies/longer wavelengths). This effect is crucial for accurately determining atomic masses and bond lengths.

(ii) Pure Vibrational Spectrum:

Pure vibrational spectra occur in the infrared (IR) region of the electromagnetic spectrum. They arise from transitions between different vibrational energy levels of a molecule while remaining in the same electronic ground state.
Condition: For a molecule to show a pure vibrational spectrum (to be IR active), it must possess a permanent dipole moment that changes during the vibration. Thus, heteronuclear diatomic molecules (like $HCl, CO$) are IR active, while homonuclear diatomics (like $N_2, O_2$) are IR inactive.

(iii) Selection Rule for Electronic Transitions:

Electronic transitions (UV-Vis spectroscopy) occur when electrons jump between different electronic molecular orbitals. The primary selection rules governing these transitions are:

  • Spin Selection Rule ($\Delta S = 0$): Transitions are only allowed between states of the same spin multiplicity (e.g., singlet to singlet, triplet to triplet). Transitions involving a change in electron spin are "forbidden."
  • Laporte Selection Rule: In molecules with a center of symmetry (centrosymmetric), transitions must involve a change in parity. Transitions between "gerade" to "ungerade" ($g \to u$ or $u \to g$) are allowed, but $g \to g$ or $u \to u$ are forbidden.
6. (a) Explain briefly the energy levels and selection rules of simple harmonic oscillator. [3][cite: 4]

Energy Levels of Simple Harmonic Oscillator (SHO):

A diatomic molecule undergoing small vibrations can be approximated as a Simple Harmonic Oscillator. Solving the Schrödinger equation gives the quantized vibrational energy levels: $$E_v = \left(v + \frac{1}{2}\right)h\nu_{osc}$$ where $v$ is the vibrational quantum number ($v = 0, 1, 2, \dots$) and $\nu_{osc}$ is the classical frequency of vibration.
Even at the lowest energy level ($v=0$), the molecule possesses energy $E_0 = \frac{1}{2}h\nu_{osc}$. This is called the Zero-Point Energy, proving that molecules are never completely at rest.

Selection Rule:

The gross selection rule requires a changing dipole moment. The specific quantum mechanical selection rule for an ideal SHO is: $$\Delta v = \pm 1$$ Meaning the molecule can only jump to the immediately adjacent upper or lower vibrational energy level.

6. (b) Prove that rotational energy of a diatomic molecule is equal to $E_{j}=\overline{B}J(J+1)cm^{-1}$. Give the selection rules for rotational spectrum. [4][cite: 4]

Proof of Rotational Energy Expression:

A diatomic molecule rotating around its center of mass is treated as a rigid rotor. The moment of inertia is $I = \mu r^2$, where $\mu$ is the reduced mass and $r$ is the bond length. According to quantum mechanics, the allowed energy states are given by solving the Schrödinger wave equation: $$E_{Joules} = \frac{h^2}{8\pi^2 I} J(J+1) \text{ Joules}$$ where $J$ is the rotational quantum number ($J = 0, 1, 2, \dots$).

In spectroscopy, energy is commonly expressed in terms of wavenumbers ($\overline{\nu}$) in $cm^{-1}$. We use the relation $E = hc\overline{\nu}$, therefore $\overline{\nu} = \frac{E}{hc}$. Dividing the energy in Joules by $hc$: $$E_J (\text{in } cm^{-1}) = \frac{h^2}{8\pi^2 I h c} J(J+1) = \frac{h}{8\pi^2 I c} J(J+1)$$

Let the constant term $\frac{h}{8\pi^2 I c}$ be defined as the rotational constant $\overline{B}$. Substituting $\overline{B}$ into the equation gives: $$E_J = \overline{B}J(J+1) \text{ cm}^{-1}$$ Hence proved.

Selection Rules for Rotational Spectrum:

The molecule must possess a permanent dipole moment to interact with the electromagnetic field (Microwave active). The specific quantum selection rule is: $$\Delta J = \pm 1$$ where $+1$ corresponds to absorption and $-1$ to emission.

7. Draw Jablonski diagram and explain different processes. What are radiative and non-radiative processes? Explain. [2+2+3=7][cite: 4]

Jablonski Diagram:

A Jablonski diagram is a state diagram that visually illustrates the electronic states of a molecule and the various transitions (processes) that can occur between them after the absorption of a photon. It maps singlet states ($S_0, S_1, S_2$) and triplet states ($T_1$).

Radiative vs Non-Radiative Processes:

When a molecule absorbs light, it gets excited to a higher state ($S_1$ or $S_2$). It dissipates this excess energy to return to the ground state ($S_0$) through two main pathways:

1. Non-Radiative Processes:
These are transitions where the excess energy is lost as heat (vibrational energy) without the emission of a photon. They are represented by wavy arrows in the diagram.

  • Internal Conversion (IC): Transition between states of the same spin multiplicity (e.g., $S_2 \to S_1$). It happens very fast ($10^{-11} s$).
  • Intersystem Crossing (ISC): Transition between states of different spin multiplicity (e.g., $S_1 \to T_1$). Because it involves a "forbidden" electron spin flip, it occurs more slowly.

2. Radiative Processes:
These are transitions where the molecule returns to the ground state by emitting the excess energy as a photon of light. They are represented by straight arrows.

  • Fluorescence: An allowed radiative transition from an excited singlet state to the ground singlet state ($S_1 \to S_0$). It is very fast ($10^{-8} s$) and stops immediately when the light source is removed.
  • Phosphorescence: A forbidden radiative transition from a triplet state to the ground singlet state ($T_1 \to S_0$). Because the electron must flip its spin back, this process is delayed, causing the material to "glow in the dark" long after the light source is removed.

8. Write short notes on the following
(i) Grothus-Drapper Law.
(ii) Photosensitized Reactions
(iii) Diamagnetism [2½+2½+2=7][cite: 4]

(i) Grotthuss-Draper Law:

Also known as the First Law of Photochemistry, it states that only the light which is absorbed by a system can cause a photochemical change. Light that is merely transmitted or reflected has no effect on the chemical bonds. This fundamental principle implies that absorption of radiation is the mandatory primary step for any photochemical reaction to occur.

(ii) Photosensitized Reactions:

In many cases, reactant molecules cannot absorb the incident light to initiate a reaction. A "photosensitizer" is a foreign substance added to the mixture that actively absorbs the light, becomes excited, and then transfers this excitation energy to the reactant molecules through collision. The reactant then undergoes the chemical reaction while the sensitizer returns to its ground state unchanged.
Example: Dissociation of $H_2$ using Mercury vapor ($Hg$) as a photosensitizer under UV light.

(iii) Diamagnetism:

Diamagnetism is a magnetic property exhibited by substances wherein all the electrons are paired. Because the spins are paired, their magnetic moments cancel out entirely. When placed in an external magnetic field, diamagnetic materials create a weak induced magnetic field in a direction opposite to the applied field, causing them to be weakly repelled by the magnet. Water, benzene, and $NaCl$ are common examples.

9. (a) Write shorts notes on the following
(i) Depression of freezing point
(ii) Abnormal molecular mass [3][cite: 4]

(i) Depression of Freezing Point:

The freezing point of a liquid is the temperature at which its liquid phase and solid phase have identical vapor pressures. When a non-volatile solute is added to a solvent, the vapor pressure of the solution becomes lower than that of the pure solvent. Consequently, the temperature must be lowered further for the solution's vapor pressure to match that of the solid solvent. This decrease in temperature is the depression of freezing point ($\Delta T_f = K_f \cdot m$).

(ii) Abnormal Molecular Mass:

Colligative properties depend on the total number of solute particles. If a solute undergoes dissociation (e.g., electrolytes like $NaCl$ splitting into ions) or association (e.g., acetic acid forming dimers) in the solution, the actual number of particles changes from the expected theoretical value. As a result, the measured colligative property deviates from theory, leading to the calculation of a molecular mass that is either significantly lower (dissociation) or higher (association) than the true formula mass. This deviation is corrected using the Van't Hoff factor ($i$).

9. (b) 10 gm of substance is dissolve in 250 ml water. Osmotic pressure of this solution is 600 mm of Hg at 15°C. Calculate molar mass of the substance. [4][cite: 4]

Given Data:

  • Mass of solute ($W_B$) = $10 \text{ g}$
  • Volume of solution ($V$) = $250 \text{ mL} = 0.25 \text{ L}$
  • Osmotic pressure ($\pi$) = $600 \text{ mm Hg} = \frac{600}{760} \text{ atm} \approx 0.7895 \text{ atm}$
  • Temperature ($T$) = $15^\circ\text{C} = 15 + 273 = 288 \text{ K}$
  • Universal Gas Constant ($R$) = $0.0821 \text{ L atm K}^{-1} \text{mol}^{-1}$

Formula:

Using the Van't Hoff equation for osmotic pressure: $\pi V = n R T = \frac{W_B}{M_B} R T$
Rearranging for molar mass ($M_B$): $$M_B = \frac{W_B \cdot R \cdot T}{\pi \cdot V}$$

Calculation:

$$M_B = \frac{10 \times 0.0821 \times 288}{0.7895 \times 0.25}$$ $$M_B = \frac{236.448}{0.197375}$$ $$M_B \approx 1197.96 \text{ g/mol}$$

Answer: The molar mass of the substance is approximately $1198 \text{ g/mol}$.

10. (a) What is osmotic pressure? How is it determined experimentally. Discuss different laws related to osmotic pressure. [1+2+2=5][cite: 4]

Osmotic Pressure:

Osmotic pressure ($\pi$) is defined as the exact amount of external mechanical pressure that must be applied to a solution side to completely stop the flow of pure solvent molecules through a semipermeable membrane into the solution.

Experimental Determination:

It is commonly determined using the Berkeley and Hartley method. In this apparatus, a porous tube coated with a semipermeable membrane (like copper ferrocyanide) holds the pure solvent. This tube is enclosed within an outer metallic jacket containing the solution. Solvent flows into the solution via osmosis, causing a capillary indicator level to drop. External pressure is then applied mechanically to the solution jacket until the capillary indicator returns to its original level. This applied pressure is read directly from a pressure gauge and equals the osmotic pressure.

Laws of Osmotic Pressure (Van't Hoff Laws):

  • Boyle-Van't Hoff Law: At constant temperature, the osmotic pressure ($\pi$) of a dilute solution is directly proportional to its concentration ($C$). ($\pi \propto C$)
  • Charles-Van't Hoff Law: For a solution of fixed concentration, the osmotic pressure is directly proportional to the absolute temperature ($T$). ($\pi \propto T$)
  • Avogadro-Van't Hoff Law: Equal volumes of all dilute solutions possessing the same osmotic pressure at the same temperature contain an equal number of solute moles.

Combining these gives the general equation: $\pi = CRT$.

10. (b) Calculate osmotic pressure of 5% urea solution at 0°C. [2][cite: 4]

Given Data:

A $5\%$ urea solution means $5 \text{ g}$ of urea is dissolved in $100 \text{ mL}$ of solution.

  • Mass of urea ($W_B$) = $5 \text{ g}$
  • Volume ($V$) = $100 \text{ mL} = 0.1 \text{ L}$
  • Temperature ($T$) = $0^\circ\text{C} = 273 \text{ K}$
  • Molar mass of urea ($M_B$ for $NH_2CONH_2$) = $14 + 2 + 12 + 16 + 14 + 2 = 60 \text{ g/mol}$
  • Gas constant ($R$) = $0.0821 \text{ L atm K}^{-1} \text{mol}^{-1}$

Calculation:

Concentration ($C$) = $\frac{\text{moles}}{\text{Volume (L)}} = \frac{5 / 60}{0.1} = \frac{0.0833}{0.1} = 0.833 \text{ M}$

Using the equation $\pi = CRT$: $$\pi = 0.833 \times 0.0821 \times 273$$ $$\pi = 0.833 \times 22.4133$$ $$\pi = 18.67 \text{ atm}$$

Answer: The osmotic pressure of the 5% urea solution is $18.67 \text{ atm}$.
