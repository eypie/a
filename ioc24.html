HSAB Concept
1
Explain Why — LiI vs LiF · CF₃I vs CF₃ · HgO vs HgS · BF₃ vs BH₃
2 + 2 + 1½ + 1½ marks
Part (a)
LiI hydrolyses easily while LiF does not.

Explanation using HSAB Principle

Li⁺ is a hard acid (small, high charge density).

F⁻ is a hard base → Li–F is a hard–hard interaction → very stable bond → LiF does not hydrolyse easily.

I⁻ is a soft base (large, polarisable) → Li–I is a hard–soft interaction → unstable, mismatched combination → LiI is easily attacked by water (hydrolysis).

Rule: Hard–hard and soft–soft combinations are stable. Hard–soft combinations are unstable and reactive.

Part (b)
Why is CF₃I less stable than CF₃?

Symbiosis Effect

In CF₃, three hard F atoms are attached to carbon. By symbiosis, these hard ligands make carbon harder. A hard carbon centre is stable.

In CF₃I, a soft I atom is bonded to the hard CF₃ carbon centre. This is a hard–soft mismatch → the C–I bond is weak and unstable.

Additionally, three strongly electron-withdrawing F atoms reduce electron density on C, making the C–I bond even weaker due to poor orbital overlap with the soft I⁻.

Bottom line: Mixing hard (CF₃ carbon) and soft (I) creates an unstable compound → CF₃I is less stable.

Part (c)
HgO is soluble in HCl but HgS is insoluble.

HSAB Explanation

Hg²⁺ is a soft acid.

O²⁻ (in HgO) is a hard base → Hg–O is a soft–hard mismatch → weak bond → HgO is easily dissolved by HCl (Cl⁻ being a borderline/soft base can displace O²⁻).

S²⁻ (in HgS) is a soft base → Hg–S is a soft–soft match → very stable bond → HgS does not dissolve even in strong acid.

HgS is one of the most insoluble salts known (Ksp ≈ 10⁻⁵²) because of the perfect soft–soft Hg²⁺–S²⁻ interaction.

Part (d)
BF₃ is a hard acid but BH₃ is a soft acid.

Effect of Substituents on Hardness/Softness of Boron

In BF₃: Three highly electronegative F atoms withdraw electron density from B → B becomes electron-poor, small effective size, high charge density → Hard acid.

In BH₃: H atoms are weakly electron-donating → B retains more electron density → B is more polarisable and has lower effective charge → Soft acid.

Key point: The nature of attached groups controls the hardness/softness of the central atom. Electron-withdrawing groups → harder; electron-donating groups → softer.

Hard & Soft Acids/Bases with Examples · Pearson's HSAB Concept
4 + 3 marks
Part (a)
Explain with examples hard and soft acids and bases.

Hard Acids

Small size, high positive charge, low polarisability, high electronegativity. Their electrons are tightly held.

  • Examples: \(\text{H}^+, \text{Li}^+, \text{Na}^+, \text{K}^+, \text{Mg}^{2+}, \text{Ca}^{2+}, \text{Al}^{3+}, \text{Fe}^{3+}, \text{BF}_3\)

Soft Acids

Large size, low or zero charge, high polarisability. Their electrons are loosely held.

  • Examples: \(\text{Cu}^+, \text{Ag}^+, \text{Au}^+, \text{Hg}^{2+}, \text{Pt}^{2+}, \text{Pd}^{2+}, \text{BH}_3, \text{I}_2\)

Hard Bases

High electronegativity donor atom, low polarisability, not easily oxidised.

  • Examples: \(\text{F}^-, \text{OH}^-, \text{H}_2\text{O}, \text{NH}_3, \text{ROH}, \text{RO}^-, \text{CO}_3^{2-}\)

Soft Bases

Low electronegativity donor atom, high polarisability, easily oxidised.

  • Examples: \(\text{I}^-, \text{S}^{2-}, \text{CN}^-, \text{CO}, \text{SCN}^-, \text{PR}_3, \text{C}_2\text{H}_4\)

Part (b)
Explain Pearson's HSAB concept.

Pearson's HSAB Principle (1963)

R.G. Pearson classified all Lewis acids and bases into Hard, Soft, and Borderline categories and stated:

"Hard acids prefer to coordinate with hard bases, and soft acids prefer to coordinate with soft bases."

Basis of the Principle

  • Hard–Hard interactions are primarily electrostatic (ionic) in nature → stable due to strong coulombic attraction.
  • Soft–Soft interactions are primarily covalent in nature → stable due to good orbital overlap and backbonding.
  • Hard–Soft combinations are weak and unstable (mismatch).

Applications

  • Predicting stability of complexes
  • Explaining solubility of salts (e.g., HgS insoluble, HgO soluble)
  • Understanding biological metal–ligand interactions
  • Predicting products of metathesis reactions

Magnetic Moment of Mn Complexes · CFSE in Tetrahedral vs Octahedral
3½ + 3 marks
Part (a)
On the basis of CFT, explain why the magnetic moment of [Mn(CN)₆]³⁻ is 2.8 BM whereas the magnetic moment of [MnBr₄]²⁻ is 5.9 BM.

Electronic Configuration of Mn

Both complexes contain \(\text{Mn}^{3+}\) and \(\text{Mn}^{2+}\). Let's identify each:

  • \([\text{Mn(CN)}_6]^{3-}\): Mn is +3, \(d^4\) configuration
  • \([\text{MnBr}_4]^{2-}\): Mn is +2, \(d^5\) configuration

[Mn(CN)₆]³⁻ — Magnetic Moment 2.8 BM

CN⁻ is a strong field ligand (high in the spectrochemical series). It causes a large crystal field splitting (\(\Delta_o\)), which overcomes the pairing energy. Electrons are forced to pair up in \(t_{2g}\) → low spin complex.

Mn³⁺ has \(d^4\): In low spin octahedral field → \(t_{2g}^4\) → 2 unpaired electrons

\[\mu = \sqrt{n(n+2)} = \sqrt{2 \times 4} = \sqrt{8} \approx 2.83 \text{ BM}\] ✓

[MnBr₄]²⁻ — Magnetic Moment 5.9 BM

Br⁻ is a weak field ligand (low in spectrochemical series). Tetrahedral splitting \(\Delta_t = \frac{4}{9}\Delta_o\) is also small → electrons remain unpaired → high spin.

Mn²⁺ has \(d^5\): In high spin → 5 unpaired electrons

\[\mu = \sqrt{5 \times 7} = \sqrt{35} \approx 5.92 \text{ BM}\] ✓

Summary: Strong ligand CN⁻ → low spin → fewer unpaired electrons → lower \(\mu\). Weak ligand Br⁻ → high spin → more unpaired electrons → higher \(\mu\).

Part (b)
CFSE is less in tetrahedral field in comparison to octahedral field.

Why CFSE is Smaller in Tetrahedral Field

Reason 1 — Fewer ligands: Octahedral has 6 ligands; tetrahedral has only 4 ligands → less total electrostatic repulsion on d-orbitals → smaller splitting.

Reason 2 — Geometry: In tetrahedral geometry, ligands do NOT point directly along any d-orbital axis. The repulsion is less effective compared to octahedral (where ligands point directly at \(d_{z^2}\) and \(d_{x^2-y^2}\)).

Mathematical Relationship:

\[\Delta_t = \frac{4}{9} \Delta_o \approx 0.44 \; \Delta_o\]

So tetrahedral CFSE is only about 44% of octahedral CFSE for the same metal and ligands.

Consequence

Because \(\Delta_t\) is small, it rarely exceeds pairing energy → tetrahedral complexes are almost always high spin (no known low-spin tetrahedral complex with common ligands).

Short Notes: Back Bonding · CFSE · Ferromagnetism · [Co(CN)₆]³⁻
1½ + 1½ + 1½ + 2 marks
Part (a)
Back bonding in complexes.

Back Bonding (π-Backbonding)

Back bonding occurs when filled d-orbitals of the metal donate electron density back into empty π* (antibonding) orbitals of the ligand. This is an additional bonding interaction beyond the normal σ-donor bond.

Condition: Ligand must have empty π* orbitals (e.g., CO, CN⁻, NO, PR₃) and metal must have d-electrons.

Example: In \(\text{Ni(CO)}_4\), the Ni→CO back bonding strengthens the Ni–C bond and weakens (lengthens) the C≡O bond. This is why the C–O stretch frequency decreases in metal carbonyls.

Back bonding explains the stability of metal carbonyls and the high position of CO in the spectrochemical series.

Part (b)
Crystal Field Stabilization Energy (CFSE).

What is CFSE?

CFSE is the extra stability gained by a metal complex due to the splitting of d-orbitals in a crystal field, compared to a hypothetical unsplit state (barycentre).

In an octahedral field: \(t_{2g}\) is lowered by \(0.4\Delta_o\) and \(e_g\) is raised by \(0.6\Delta_o\).

\[\text{CFSE} = (-0.4 \times n_{t_{2g}} + 0.6 \times n_{e_g}) \times \Delta_o\]

Example: \(d^6\) low spin in octahedral: \(t_{2g}^6\) → CFSE = \(-0.4 \times 6 = -2.4\Delta_o\) (highly stable)

Higher CFSE → more stable complex. CFSE explains Irving-Williams series, lattice energies, and colour of complexes.

Part (c)
Ferromagnetism and Antiferromagnetism.

Ferromagnetism

All unpaired electron spins are aligned parallel in small domains → extremely strong attraction to magnetic field. This persists even after removing the external field (permanent magnetism).

Examples: Fe, Co, Ni, Gd, and their alloys.

Antiferromagnetism

Unpaired electron spins on adjacent atoms are aligned antiparallel → they cancel each other out → net magnetic moment is zero (or very small).

Examples: MnO, FeO, NiO, Cr₂O₃.

Key difference: Ferromagnetic: parallel spins → strong. Antiferromagnetic: antiparallel spins → cancel → weak/zero.

Part (d)
Magnetic behaviour of [Co(CN)₆]³⁻ ion.

Analysis

In \([\text{Co(CN)}_6]^{3-}\), Co is in the +3 oxidation state → \(\text{Co}^{3+}\) → electron configuration \(d^6\).

CN⁻ is a strong field ligand → causes large \(\Delta_o\) → all 6 d-electrons pair up in \(t_{2g}\):

\[t_{2g}^6 \; e_g^0\]

→ Zero unpaired electrons

Result: \([\text{Co(CN)}_6]^{3-}\) is diamagnetic. \(\mu = \sqrt{0(0+2)} = 0\) BM. It is repelled by a magnetic field.

This is one of the most studied examples of a low-spin octahedral complex.

Short Notes: Charge Transfer Spectra · Spectrochemical Series · Spin Selection Rules
2 + 2 + 2½ marks
Part (a)
Charge transfer spectra.

What is Charge Transfer (CT) Spectra?

Charge transfer transitions occur when an electron is transferred from the ligand to the metal (LMCT) or from the metal to the ligand (MLCT) upon absorption of UV/visible light.

Types

  • LMCT (Ligand → Metal): Ligand donates electron to metal. Common when metal is in high oxidation state and ligand is easily oxidised. Example: \(\text{MnO}_4^-\) (permanganate's intense purple colour — LMCT from O²⁻ to Mn⁷⁺)
  • MLCT (Metal → Ligand): Metal donates electron to ligand. Common when metal has low oxidation state and ligand has low-lying π* orbitals. Example: \([\text{Ru(bpy)}_3]^{2+}\)

Key Feature: CT bands are very intense (ε ~ 10⁴ L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹) — much more intense than d–d bands — because they are Laporte-allowed.

Part (b)
Spectrochemical series.

Spectrochemical Series

An empirical ordering of ligands by their ability to split d-orbitals (i.e., the value of \(\Delta\) they produce). Arranged from weakest to strongest field:

\[\text{I}^- < \text{Br}^- < \text{S}^{2-} < \text{Cl}^- < \text{F}^- < \text{OH}^- < \text{H}_2\text{O} < \text{SCN}^- < \text{NH}_3 < \text{en} < \text{NO}_2^- < \text{CN}^- \approx \text{CO}\]

Applications

  • Predicting whether a complex is high spin or low spin
  • Explaining colour differences in similar complexes
  • Rationalising CFSE values

Example: \([\text{CoF}_6]^{3-}\) (F⁻ = weak field) is high spin; \([\text{Co(CN)}_6]^{3-}\) (CN⁻ = strong field) is low spin.

Part (c)
Spin selection rules.

Spin Selection Rule

The spin selection rule states: Electronic transitions are only allowed between states of the same spin multiplicity.

\[\Delta S = 0\]

This means during a d–d transition, the total spin of the system must not change. Spin-allowed transitions occur between states with the same number of unpaired electrons.

Examples

  • Allowed: ²T₂g → ²Eg (both doublet states, \(\Delta S = 0\))
  • Forbidden: ⁶A₁g → ⁴T₁g (\(\Delta S \neq 0\), spin changes)

Spin-Forbidden Transitions

They still appear but are very weak (ε ~ 0.001–1 L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹). Spin-orbit coupling causes slight mixing of spin states, allowing these "forbidden" bands to appear faintly.

Combined with Laporte rule: d–d transitions are both spin and Laporte-forbidden → very weak absorption bands in transition metal complexes.

Nucleophilic Substitution in Square Planar Complexes — Associative Mechanism
6½ marks

Nucleophilic Substitution in Square Planar Complexes

Square planar complexes (mainly Pt²⁺, Pd²⁺, Au³⁺) undergo ligand substitution reactions. The incoming ligand (nucleophile) replaces an existing ligand.

\[[\text{PtL}_4]^{2+} + Y \to [\text{PtL}_3Y]^{2+} + L\]

Associative (Ia) Mechanism

Square planar substitution proceeds via an associative or interchange-associative (Ia) mechanism, NOT dissociative (Id). This means the incoming nucleophile attacks first before the leaving group departs.

  1. Step 1 — Attack: The nucleophile Y approaches the central metal from above or below the plane (square planar has open axial positions). A 5-coordinate trigonal bipyramidal (TBP) intermediate is formed.
  2. Step 2 — Release: The leaving group X departs from the TBP intermediate → product square planar complex is formed.
\[[\text{PtX(A)(B)(C)}] + Y \to [\text{Pt(Y)(X)(A)(B)(C)}]^{\dagger} \to [\text{PtY(A)(B)(C)}] + X\]

† = 5-coordinate trigonal bipyramidal transition state

Why Associative Mechanism? (Not Dissociative)

  • Open coordination sites: Square planar metal has two accessible axial positions → easy for the nucleophile to approach without steric hindrance.
  • Rate depends on nucleophile: The rate of reaction changes with the nature of the incoming nucleophile (Y) — a clear sign of associative mechanism. In a dissociative mechanism, the rate would be independent of Y.
  • Electron-rich metal: Pt²⁺ has \(d^8\) configuration with filled d-orbitals → can accept a nucleophile without fully releasing one first.
  • Trans effect influences selectivity: Ligands in the trans position to the leaving group accelerate its departure — further evidence of an associative pathway.

Rate Law: \[\text{Rate} = k_1[\text{complex}] + k_2[\text{complex}][Y]\] The \(k_2\) term (nucleophile-dependent) confirms the associative pathway.

Nucleophilicity Order for Pt²⁺

\[\text{F}^- < \text{Cl}^- < \text{Br}^- < \text{I}^- < \text{CN}^- \approx \text{CO} < \text{PR}_3\]

Hydrogenation by Homogeneous Catalyst · [Fe(CO)₅] Structure · 18e Rule
2½ + 2½ + 2 marks
Part (a)
The mechanism of hydrogenation of alkene by homogeneous catalyst.

Wilkinson's Catalyst

The most important homogeneous hydrogenation catalyst is Wilkinson's Catalyst: \(\text{RhCl(PPh}_3)_3\). It dissolves in the reaction solvent (homogeneous).

Mechanism

  1. Dissociation: One PPh₃ leaves → 14-electron active species \([\text{RhCl(PPh}_3)_2]\)
  2. Oxidative Addition of H₂: H₂ adds to Rh → \(\text{Rh}^+\) becomes \(\text{Rh}^{3+}\) — two H atoms now on metal
  3. Alkene Coordination: The alkene (e.g., CH₂=CH₂) coordinates to Rh
  4. Migratory Insertion: One H migrates to the alkene (hydrometallation) → alkyl complex
  5. Reductive Elimination: Second H transfers to the alkyl → alkane product is released → Rh returns to +1 state
\[\text{CH}_2{=}\text{CH}_2 + \text{H}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{Wilkinson's catalyst}} \text{CH}_3\text{CH}_3\]

Key feature: The reaction is stereospecific — both H atoms add to the same face of the alkene (syn addition).

Part (b)
Structure and bonding in [Fe(CO)₅] complex.

Iron Pentacarbonyl — [Fe(CO)₅]

Structure: \([\text{Fe(CO)}_5]\) has a trigonal bipyramidal geometry — 3 CO in the equatorial plane and 2 CO in the axial positions. All 5 CO are equivalent due to Berry pseudorotation in solution.

Bonding

  • σ-donation: Each CO donates a lone pair (from carbon) into an empty d-orbital of Fe → forms a dative σ-bond (CO is a σ-donor)
  • π-backbonding: Filled Fe d-orbitals donate electron density back into empty CO π* orbitals → strengthens Fe–C bond and weakens C≡O bond

Fe is in 0 oxidation state (d⁸). Total electron count: Fe(0) = 8e, 5 CO = 5×2 = 10e → total = 18 electrons (obeys 18e rule)

Evidence: C–O stretching frequency in \([\text{Fe(CO)}_5]\) (~2013 cm⁻¹) is lower than in free CO (2143 cm⁻¹) — confirms π-backbonding weakens the C≡O bond.

Part (c)
18-electron rule.

The 18-Electron Rule

The 18-electron rule (also called the EAN rule — Effective Atomic Number rule) states: Stable transition metal complexes tend to have 18 electrons in the valence shell of the metal (9 orbitals: 1s + 3p + 5d).

This achieves the electron configuration of the next noble gas.

Counting Electrons

  • Metal contributes its d-electrons (use ionic or covalent counting)
  • Each ligand contributes 2 electrons (for 2-electron donors like CO, PR₃, H₂O)

Examples

ComplexMetal e⁻Ligand e⁻Total
\(\text{Ni(CO)}_4\)10 (Ni⁰)4×2 = 818 ✓
\(\text{Fe(CO)}_5\)8 (Fe⁰)5×2 = 1018 ✓
\(\text{Cr(CO)}_6\)6 (Cr⁰)6×2 = 1218 ✓

Exceptions: Square planar \(d^8\) complexes (e.g., \([\text{PtCl}_4]^{2-}\)) are stable with 16e. Very bulky ligands can give 16e complexes due to steric reasons.

Alkyl Lithium as Super Grignard · Uses of Organometallic Complexes
3½ + 3 marks
Part (a)
Why are alkyl lithium compounds known as Super Grignard's reagent? How does methyl lithium react with CO₂, R–CH₂–OH, and HCHO?

Why "Super Grignard"?

Alkyl lithium compounds (R–Li) are called Super Grignard reagents because:

  • The C–Li bond is more ionic and more polar than the C–Mg bond in Grignard reagents (RMgX).
  • They are stronger nucleophiles and stronger bases than Grignard reagents.
  • They react with substrates that Grignard reagents cannot, including esters, nitriles, and even simple alkanes under certain conditions.
  • They can deprotonate very weakly acidic C–H bonds.

Reactions of Methyl Lithium (CH₃Li)

(i) With CO₂:

\[\text{CH}_3\text{Li} + \text{CO}_2 \to \text{CH}_3\text{COOLi} \xrightarrow{\text{H}_3\text{O}^+} \text{CH}_3\text{COOH}\]

Nucleophilic addition to CO₂ → gives acetic acid (carboxylic acid with one more carbon).

(ii) With R–CH₂–OH (primary alcohol):

\[\text{CH}_3\text{Li} + \text{R-CH}_2\text{-OH} \to \text{CH}_4 + \text{R-CH}_2\text{-OLi}\]

CH₃Li is a strong base — it deprotonates the O–H bond of the alcohol → gives methane gas and lithium alkoxide. This is an acid–base reaction, not addition.

(iii) With HCHO (formaldehyde):

\[\text{CH}_3\text{Li} + \text{HCHO} \to \text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{OLi} \xrightarrow{\text{H}_3\text{O}^+} \text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{OH}\]

Nucleophilic addition to the carbonyl → gives ethanol (primary alcohol).

Part (b)
Write an essay on uses of organometallic complexes.

Uses of Organometallic Complexes

1. Industrial Catalysis

  • Wacker Process: Pd²⁺ catalyst converts ethylene to acetaldehyde → \(\text{CH}_2{=}\text{CH}_2 + \frac{1}{2}\text{O}_2 \to \text{CH}_3\text{CHO}\)
  • Ziegler-Natta Polymerisation: Ti and Al organometallic catalysts produce polyethylene and polypropylene (used in plastics)
  • Monsanto Process: Rh carbonyl catalyst for acetic acid production from methanol and CO

2. Hydroformylation (Oxo Process)

Cobalt or Rh carbonyls convert alkenes + CO + H₂ into aldehydes. Used to make plasticisers and detergents.

3. Medicine

  • Cisplatin \(\text{[PtCl}_2(\text{NH}_3)_2]\) — landmark anticancer drug
  • Ferrocene derivatives — antimalarial and antibacterial agents
  • Organogold compounds — used in rheumatoid arthritis treatment

4. Organic Synthesis

  • Grignard and organolithium reagents — form C–C bonds (essential in synthesis)
  • Palladium-catalysed cross-coupling (Suzuki, Heck reactions) — makes complex drug molecules

5. Semiconductors & Materials

Organometallic vapour phase epitaxy (OMVPE) uses compounds like trimethylgallium to deposit semiconductor thin films (LED production).

Chlorophyll — Structure & Functions · Nitrogen Fixation
3½ + 3 marks
Part (a)
Write the structure and functions of chlorophyll.

Structure of Chlorophyll

Chlorophyll is a magnesium-containing porphyrin complex. Its key structural features:

  • Central Mg²⁺ ion coordinated to 4 nitrogen atoms of a porphyrin ring (tetrapyrrole system)
  • The porphyrin ring is highly conjugated → absorbs visible light
  • A phytol chain (long hydrophobic tail) is attached — anchors chlorophyll to the thylakoid membrane
  • A cyclopentanone ring (ring V) is fused to one of the pyrrole rings
  • Chlorophyll a: has a –CH₃ group; Chlorophyll b: has a –CHO group at the same position

Absorption: Chlorophyll a absorbs at ~430 nm (blue) and ~662 nm (red). Chlorophyll b absorbs at ~453 nm and ~642 nm. They appear green because green light (~550 nm) is reflected.

Functions of Chlorophyll

  • Light harvesting: Absorbs sunlight energy in photosynthesis
  • Energy transfer: Transfers absorbed energy to the reaction centre (P680 in PS II, P700 in PS I)
  • Charge separation: Excited electron is transferred to an acceptor molecule, initiating the electron transport chain
  • Overall: Converts light energy into chemical energy stored as glucose: \[6\text{CO}_2 + 6\text{H}_2\text{O} \xrightarrow{\text{light, chlorophyll}} \text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6 + 6\text{O}_2\]
Part (b)
Explain the phenomenon of nitrogen fixation.

Nitrogen Fixation

Nitrogen fixation is the process of converting atmospheric dinitrogen (\(\text{N}_2\)) into biologically usable forms like \(\text{NH}_3\) or \(\text{NO}_3^-\). N₂ is very inert (strong N≡N triple bond, bond energy = 945 kJ/mol).

Types

  • Biological fixation: Carried out by nitrogen-fixing bacteria (Rhizobium, Azotobacter, cyanobacteria) using the enzyme nitrogenase
  • Industrial fixation (Haber process): \(\text{N}_2 + 3\text{H}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{Fe catalyst, 450°C, 200 atm}} 2\text{NH}_3\)

Nitrogenase Enzyme

Nitrogenase has two protein components:

  • Fe-protein (dinitrogenase reductase): Contains an Fe₄S₄ cluster. Uses ATP to transfer electrons.
  • MoFe-protein (dinitrogenase): Contains the active site — FeMo-cofactor (FeMoco) — where N₂ is bound and reduced.
\[\text{N}_2 + 8\text{H}^+ + 8e^- + 16\text{ATP} \xrightarrow{\text{nitrogenase}} 2\text{NH}_3 + \text{H}_2 + 16\text{ADP} + 16\text{P}_i\]

Key point: Nitrogenase requires strictly anaerobic conditions — O₂ irreversibly destroys the enzyme. Leguminous plants have leghemoglobin (pink protein) to maintain low O₂ levels in root nodules.

Silicones — What They Are, Synthesis, and Structure
6½ marks

What are Silicones?

Silicones (polysiloxanes) are synthetic polymers with a silicon–oxygen backbone (–Si–O–Si–O–) with organic groups (usually –CH₃) attached to silicon. They have unique properties combining inorganic stability with organic flexibility.

General formula: \([R_2\text{SiO}]_n\) where R = organic group (commonly CH₃, C₂H₅, or phenyl)

Synthesis of Silicones

Step 1 — Preparation of chlorosilanes (Rochow process):

\[\text{Si} + 2\text{CH}_3\text{Cl} \xrightarrow{\text{Cu catalyst, 300°C}} (\text{CH}_3)_2\text{SiCl}_2\]

Other products also formed: CH₃SiCl₃ (trifunctional), (CH₃)₃SiCl (monofunctional).

Step 2 — Hydrolysis of chlorosilanes:

\[(\text{CH}_3)_2\text{SiCl}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} \to (\text{CH}_3)_2\text{Si(OH)}_2 + 2\text{HCl}\]

Step 3 — Condensation (Polymerisation):

\[n(\text{CH}_3)_2\text{Si(OH)}_2 \to [\text{(CH}_3)_2\text{SiO}]_n + n\text{H}_2\text{O}\]

Chain length and cross-linking is controlled by the ratio of mono-, di-, and trifunctional monomers.

Types and Structures

TypeMonomerStructureProduct
Linear (oil/fluid)R₂SiCl₂ (difunctional)Linear chain –(R₂SiO)ₙ–Silicone oils, lubricants
CyclicR₂SiCl₂ in special conditionsRing structure (SiO)ₙCyclosiloxanes
Rubbery (elastomer)Long chains + cross-linkingLightly cross-linked networkSilicone rubber, seals
ResinousRSiCl₃ + R₂SiCl₂3D network (highly cross-linked)Hard resins, coatings

Properties and Uses

  • Excellent thermal stability (–60°C to +250°C)
  • Water-repellent (hydrophobic)
  • Chemically inert and non-toxic
  • Good electrical insulators
  • Uses: Lubricants, antifoaming agents, waterproofing, sealants, medical implants, cookware (non-stick), electrical insulation

Why Si–O backbone? The Si–O–Si bond angle (~145°) and bond energy (~450 kJ/mol) give silicones their flexibility and thermal stability — much better than C–C polymer backbones at high temperatures.
