Unit I — Hard & Soft Acids and Bases (HSAB)
Q1 Explain why (four sub-parts on HSAB & bonding) 7 marks ▼
(a) 2 marks
LiI hydrolyses easily while LiF does not.

Key idea: This is explained using Fajan's Rules and the HSAB principle.

Li⁺ is a small, hard acid. F⁻ is a small, hard base → Hard-Hard combination → strong ionic bond → LiF is stable and does not hydrolyse easily.
I⁻ is a large, soft base paired with hard Li⁺ → Hard-Soft combination → the bond is weaker and more polarisable → water molecules easily attack → LiI hydrolyses readily.

Additionally, LiI has more covalent character (large I⁻ is highly polarisable by Li⁺), making it more reactive with polar solvents like water.

(b) 2 marks
Why is CF₃I less stable than CF₃F (i.e., CF₄)?

In CF₄, carbon is bonded to four fluorine atoms. F is a small, hard base and C (in CF₄) is a hard acid → excellent match → very strong and stable C–F bonds.

In CF₃I, iodine is a large, soft, polarisable atom bonded to a relatively hard carbon. The C–I bond is weak and long, making CF₃I less stable and more reactive (C–I bond energy ≈ 213 kJ/mol vs C–F ≈ 485 kJ/mol).

Summary: Hard C prefers Hard F (HSAB match) over Soft I → CF₄ >> CF₃I in stability.
(c) 1½ marks
HgO is soluble in HCl but HgS is insoluble.

Hg²⁺ is a soft acid (large, polarisable, low charge density).

  • O²⁻ is a hard base → Soft-Hard pair in HgO → weak interaction → HgO can dissolve in HCl.
  • S²⁻ is a soft base → Soft-Soft pair in HgS → very strong, stable interaction → HgS is extremely insoluble (Ksp ≈ 10⁻⁵²).
HSAB rule: Soft acid (Hg²⁺) + Soft base (S²⁻) = very stable compound → insoluble in HCl.
(d) 1½ marks
BF₃ is a hard acid but BH₃ is a soft acid.
PropertyBF₃ (Hard)BH₃ (Soft)
Attached atomsF (highly electronegative)H (weakly electronegative)
Charge on BHigh positive (F pulls e⁻)Low positive (H donates e⁻)
PolarisabilityLow (small effective size)Higher (more diffuse e⁻ cloud)
HSAB classHard acidSoft acid

F atoms withdraw electron density from B → B becomes small & highly charged → Hard. H atoms donate e⁻ density to B → B becomes more polarisable → Soft.

Q2 Hard & Soft Acids/Bases with examples + Pearson's HSAB concept 7 marks ▼
(a) 4 marks — Hard & Soft Acids and Bases

Pearson (1963) classified Lewis acids and bases as Hard, Soft, or Borderline based on their polarisability and charge density.

Hard Acid: Small size, high positive charge, low polarisability, no easily excitable outer electrons.
Examples: H⁺, Li⁺, Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺, BF₃, Fe³⁺
Soft Acid: Large size, low or zero charge, high polarisability, easily accessible d-orbitals.
Examples: Cu⁺, Ag⁺, Hg²⁺, Pd²⁺, Pt²⁺, BH₃
Hard Base: Small size, high electronegativity, low polarisability, hard to oxidise.
Examples: F⁻, OH⁻, H₂O, NH₃, CO₃²⁻
Soft Base: Large size, low electronegativity, highly polarisable, easily oxidised.
Examples: I⁻, S²⁻, R₂S, CN⁻, CO, R₃P
HSAB Principle: Hard acids prefer to bind with hard bases, and soft acids prefer to bind with soft bases — forming the most stable complexes.
(b) 3 marks — Pearson's HSAB Concept

Pearson's HSAB (Hard and Soft Acids and Bases) concept is a qualitative theory to predict:

  • Stability of acid-base complexes
  • Solubility of ionic compounds
  • Reaction pathways

Rule: "Hard likes Hard, Soft likes Soft"

Applications:

  1. Solubility: AgF (soft-hard) is soluble; AgI (soft-soft) is insoluble.
  2. Stability of complexes: Fe³⁺ (hard) forms stable complex with F⁻ (hard) but weak complex with I⁻ (soft). Fe²⁺ (softer) forms more stable complex with CN⁻.
  3. Biological relevance: Ca²⁺ (hard) binds O-donor ligands in bones; Pt²⁺ (soft) binds N in DNA (basis of anticancer drugs like cisplatin).
Unit II — Crystal Field Theory (CFT)
Q3 Crystal Field Theory — magnetic moments and CFSE 6½ marks ▼
(a) 3½ marks
Magnetic moment of [Mn(CN)₆]³⁻ is 2.8 BM whereas [MnBr₄]²⁻ is 5.9 BM.

Mn³⁺ has electronic configuration [Ar] 3d⁴ → 4 d-electrons.

Magnetic moment formula: \(\mu = \sqrt{n(n+2)}\) BM, where n = number of unpaired electrons.

[Mn(CN)₆]³⁻ — Octahedral, Strong Field Ligand (CN⁻)
CN⁻ is a strong field ligand → causes large crystal field splitting (\(\Delta_o\)).
4 electrons pair up in t₂g level: t₂g⁴ eg⁰ → 2 unpaired electrons.
\(\mu = \sqrt{2(2+2)} = \sqrt{8} \approx 2.83\) BM ✓ (Low spin)
[MnBr₄]²⁻ — Tetrahedral, Weak Field Ligand (Br⁻)
Mn²⁺ in tetrahedral field with weak Br⁻ ligands → very small crystal field splitting (\(\Delta_t\)).
Note: Mn here is Mn²⁺ (d⁵ configuration) → 5 unpaired electrons in high spin.
\(\mu = \sqrt{5(5+2)} = \sqrt{35} \approx 5.92\) BM ✓ (High spin)

Since \(\Delta_t \approx \frac{4}{9}\Delta_o\), tetrahedral complexes almost always remain high spin.

(b) 3 marks
CFSE is less in tetrahedral field than in octahedral field.

Crystal Field Stabilisation Energy (CFSE) depends on how much the d-electrons are stabilised below the barycentre (energy centre).

  • In octahedral field: splitting energy = \(\Delta_o\). The t₂g orbitals are stabilised by \(-0.4\Delta_o\) each.
  • In tetrahedral field: splitting energy = \(\Delta_t = \frac{4}{9}\Delta_o \approx 0.44\Delta_o\).
Two reasons CFSE is less in tetrahedral:
1. \(\Delta_t\) is only ~4/9 of \(\Delta_o\) — inherently smaller splitting.
2. There are only 4 ligands in tetrahedral vs 6 in octahedral — less electrostatic interaction.
3. The e orbitals (lower set) are stabilised by \(-0.6\Delta_t\), but since \(\Delta_t\) itself is small, the total CFSE is much smaller.
Q4 Short notes — Back bonding, CFSE, Ferromagnetism, [Co(CN)₆]³⁻ 6½ marks ▼
(a) 1½ marks — Back Bonding in Complexes

Back bonding (π back-bonding) occurs when a metal donates electron density from its filled d-orbitals to empty π* (antibonding) or p orbitals of the ligand.

Example: In Ni(CO)₄ — Ni donates e⁻ from d-orbitals into empty π* of CO.
This strengthens the M–L bond and weakens the C≡O bond (CO stretching frequency decreases).

Back bonding explains why CO, CN⁻, and NO are such strong field ligands — they remove electron density from the metal, reducing d-d repulsion.

(b) 1½ marks — Crystal Field Stabilisation Energy (CFSE)

CFSE is the extra stability gained by a transition metal complex due to splitting of d-orbitals in a crystal field.

Formula (Octahedral):
CFSE = (no. of e⁻ in t₂g × (−0.4Δₒ)) + (no. of e⁻ in eₘ × (+0.6Δₒ))

Example: d⁶ low spin: t₂g⁶ eₘ⁰ → CFSE = 6 × (−0.4) = −2.4Δₒ (very stable — explains inertness of Co³⁺ complexes)

(c) 1½ marks — Ferromagnetism & Antiferromagnetism

(d) 2 marks — Magnetic behaviour of [Co(CN)₆]³⁻

Co³⁺ has electronic configuration [Ar] 3d⁶ → 6 d-electrons.

CN⁻ is a strong field ligand → large Δₒ → low spin configuration.

Configuration: t₂g⁶ eₘ⁰
All 6 electrons are paired in t₂g → 0 unpaired electrons
\(\mu = \sqrt{0(0+2)} = 0\) BM
Therefore [Co(CN)₆]³⁻ is diamagnetic (not attracted to magnetic field).

This is a classic example of a low-spin d⁶ octahedral complex with maximum CFSE = −2.4Δₒ.

Unit III — Electronic Spectra of Complexes
Q5 Charge transfer spectra, Spectrochemical series, Spin selection rules 6½ marks ▼
(a) 2 marks — Charge Transfer Spectra

Charge Transfer (CT) spectra arise when an electron is transferred between the metal and ligand — NOT within the metal d-orbitals.

Two types:
• LMCT (Ligand-to-Metal): Electron moves from ligand orbital to metal d-orbital. Common in complexes with oxidising metals (e.g., [MnO₄]⁻ — purple colour).
• MLCT (Metal-to-Ligand): Electron moves from metal to ligand π* orbital. Common with strong π-acceptor ligands (e.g., [Fe(bipy)₃]²⁺ — red colour).

CT bands are very intense (ε = 10³–10⁵ L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹) because they are spin and symmetry allowed. This is why KMnO₄ and K₂Cr₂O₇ are intensely coloured.

(b) 2 marks — Spectrochemical Series

The spectrochemical series arranges ligands in order of their ability to split the d-orbitals (i.e., increasing Δ value):

I⁻ < Br⁻ < S²⁻ < SCN⁻ < Cl⁻ < F⁻ < OH⁻ < C₂O₄²⁻ < H₂O < NCS⁻ < NH₃ < en < bipy < NO₂⁻ < CN⁻ < CO
  • Weak field ligands (left side): small Δ, high spin complexes, less CFSE.
  • Strong field ligands (right side): large Δ, low spin complexes, more CFSE.

CO and CN⁻ are strongest because they participate in back bonding, stabilising the metal-ligand interaction.

(c) 2½ marks — Spin Selection Rules

For an electronic transition in a complex to be allowed (intense absorption), two selection rules must be followed:

1. Spin Selection Rule (ΔS = 0):
A transition is only allowed if the total spin quantum number does NOT change.
→ Transitions between states of the same spin multiplicity are allowed.
→ Singlet→Singlet ✓ allowed; Triplet→Singlet ✗ forbidden.
2. Laporte (Orbital) Selection Rule (Δl = ±1):
Transitions between orbitals of the same parity (g→g or u→u) are forbidden.
d→d transitions are forbidden (g→g), which is why d-d bands are weak (ε ≈ 10–100).
CT transitions are allowed (g→u), hence much more intense.

Spin-forbidden transitions can still occur weakly due to spin-orbit coupling. Vibronic coupling can relax the Laporte rule, which is why octahedral complexes show weak colour despite forbidden d-d transitions.

Q6 Nucleophilic substitution in square planar complexes — mechanism & why associative 6½ marks ▼

Square planar complexes (e.g., [Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂]) undergo nucleophilic substitution via the Associative (A) or Ia mechanism.

General Reaction:
[ML₃X] + Y → [ML₃Y] + X
(X = leaving group, Y = incoming nucleophile)

Steps of the Associative Mechanism:

  1. Attack: The incoming nucleophile (Y) attacks the metal centre from above or below the plane, forming a 5-coordinate trigonal bipyramidal or square pyramidal intermediate.
  2. Intermediate: A 5-coordinate, 18-electron species forms momentarily — this is the activated complex.
  3. Departure: The leaving group (X) leaves, regenerating a 4-coordinate square planar product.
Why Associative mechanism?
1. Vacant axial positions: Square planar complexes have empty positions above and below the plane — easy access for nucleophile.
2. 16-electron count: Square planar complexes are electron-deficient (only 16 electrons) → readily accept an incoming ligand to reach 18e.
3. d⁸ metals like Pt²⁺, Pd²⁺, Rh⁺ have filled dz² orbital oriented perpendicular to the plane — repels axial approach but still allows nucleophilic attack from an angle.
4. Rate depends on the nature of the nucleophile (nucleophilicity parameter, n) — confirming associative pathway.

Trans effect: The leaving group is always trans to the strongest trans-directing ligand (CO > CN⁻ > Cl⁻ > NH₃), which weakens the trans M–L bond and assists substitution.

Unit IV — Organometallic Chemistry
Q7 Homogeneous hydrogenation of alkenes; [Fe(CO)₅] structure; 18e rule 6½ marks ▼
(a) 2½ marks — Mechanism of Hydrogenation of Alkene by Homogeneous Catalyst

Wilkinson's catalyst — RhCl(PPh₃)₃ — is the most common homogeneous catalyst for alkene hydrogenation. The mechanism involves:

  1. Dissociation: One PPh₃ leaves to generate a 14e, coordinatively unsaturated Rh(I) complex.
  2. Oxidative Addition of H₂: H₂ adds across Rh — Rh(I) becomes Rh(III), forming a dihydride complex.
  3. Alkene Coordination: The alkene binds to the metal centre (π-complex).
  4. Migratory Insertion: One H migrates to the coordinated alkene (hydrometallation) → alkyl-metal complex.
  5. Reductive Elimination: The second H transfers to the alkyl group, releasing the saturated alkane and regenerating Rh(I) catalyst.
RhCl(PPh₃)₃ + H₂ → H₂RhCl(PPh₃)₃ H₂RhCl(PPh₃)₃ + alkene → alkane + RhCl(PPh₃)₃
(b) 2 marks — Structure and Bonding in [Fe(CO)₅]

Iron pentacarbonyl is the most well-known metal carbonyl.

Structure: Trigonal Bipyramidal (D₃h symmetry)
— 3 CO in equatorial positions
— 2 CO in axial positions
Fe–C≡O bond angle: 180° (linear CO)
Geometry: Fe at centre, 5 CO arranged as TBP

Bonding involves two components:

  • σ-donation: CO donates lone pair from carbon to empty d/sp³d orbital of Fe → forms σ-bond.
  • π-back bonding: Fe donates e⁻ from filled d-orbitals into empty CO π* orbital → strengthens M–C bond, weakens C≡O (observed by lower IR stretching frequency ~2013 cm⁻¹ vs free CO at 2143 cm⁻¹).

Electron count: Fe(0) = 8 e⁻; 5 CO × 2 = 10 e⁻ → total = 18 electrons ✓

(c) 2 marks — 18-Electron Rule

The 18-electron rule (Effective Atomic Number rule) states that stable transition metal complexes tend to have exactly 18 electrons in their valence shell (like noble gases).

Why 18? Transition metals have 9 valence orbitals (1 × s + 3 × p + 5 × d) → can accommodate 18 electrons.

Examples:

  • [Fe(CO)₅]: Fe(0) = 8 + 5×2 = 18 ✓
  • [Cr(CO)₆]: Cr(0) = 6 + 6×2 = 18 ✓
  • [Mn₂(CO)₁₀]: each Mn = 7 + 5×2 + 1 (M–M bond) = 18 ✓

Complexes with 18e are kinetically inert and thermodynamically stable. 16e complexes (like Pt²⁺, Pd²⁺) are reactive and undergo associative substitution.

Q8 Alkyl lithium as Super Grignard + Uses of Organometallic Complexes 6½ marks ▼
(a) 3½ marks — Alkyl Lithium Compounds

Alkyl lithium compounds (R–Li, e.g., CH₃Li, n-BuLi) are called "Super Grignard" reagents because they are much more reactive than Grignard reagents (RMgX). This is because the C–Li bond is more ionic/polar than C–Mg bond, making the carbanion (R⁻) more nucleophilic and basic.

Reactions of Methyl Lithium (CH₃Li):

(i) With CO₂:
CH₃Li + CO₂ → CH₃COOLi (lithium acetate)
On hydrolysis → CH₃COOH (acetic acid)
CH₃⁻ acts as nucleophile, attacks electrophilic carbon of CO₂
(ii) With R–CH₂–OH (primary alcohol):
CH₃Li + R–CH₂–OH → CH₄ + R–CH₂–OLi
Strong base reaction — CH₃⁻ deprotonates the alcohol O–H bond (pKa ~16), releasing methane.
(iii) With HCHO (formaldehyde):
CH₃Li + HCHO → CH₃CH₂OLi → (hydrolysis) → CH₃CH₂OH (ethanol)
Nucleophilic addition to carbonyl — gives a primary alcohol one carbon longer.
(b) 3 marks — Uses of Organometallic Complexes
  1. Catalysis: Ziegler-Natta catalyst [TiCl₄/AlEt₃] for polymerisation of ethylene and propylene → industrial production of polyethylene and polypropylene.
  2. Hydroformylation (Oxo Process): [HCo(CO)₄] converts alkenes + CO + H₂ into aldehydes/alcohols — important in chemical industry.
  3. Medicine: Cisplatin [Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂] is an anticancer drug; ferrocene derivatives have antitumour and antimalarial activity.
  4. Organic Synthesis: Grignard reagents and organolithiums are fundamental tools in forming C–C bonds.
  5. Monsanto Acetic Acid Process: [Rh(CO)₂I₂]⁻ catalyst converts methanol + CO → acetic acid (carbonylation).
Unit V — Bioinorganic Chemistry & Silicones
Q9 Structure & functions of chlorophyll + Nitrogen fixation 6½ marks ▼
(a) 3½ marks — Chlorophyll: Structure & Functions

Chlorophyll is the green pigment in plants responsible for photosynthesis. It is a magnesium porphyrin complex.

Structure:
• Central metal: Mg²⁺ (coordinated to 4 nitrogen atoms of porphyrin ring)
• Core: A porphyrin ring (tetrapyrrole macrocycle) — highly conjugated → green colour
• Ring V: An additional cyclopentanone ring (isocyclic ring)
• Long hydrophobic phytol tail (C₂₀ chain) anchors it in the thylakoid membrane
• Chlorophyll a: has –CH₃ group at C-3
• Chlorophyll b: has –CHO group at C-3 (absorbs different wavelengths)

Functions of Chlorophyll:

  1. Light absorption: Absorbs red (~680 nm) and blue (~430 nm) light; reflects green — that's why plants look green.
  2. Energy transfer: Transfers absorbed light energy to the reaction centre (P680 in PSII, P700 in PSI).
  3. Charge separation: At the reaction centre, excited chlorophyll donates an electron — initiating the photochemical reaction of photosynthesis.
  4. Role in O₂ evolution: Chlorophyll a in PSII drives water splitting (2H₂O → 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ + O₂).
(b) 3 marks — Nitrogen Fixation

Nitrogen fixation is the process of converting atmospheric N₂ (very stable, triple bond) into ammonia (NH₃) or other nitrogen compounds usable by living organisms.

Biological Nitrogen Fixation:
Carried out by microorganisms (e.g., Rhizobium, Azotobacter) using the enzyme Nitrogenase.

\[N_2 + 8H^+ + 8e^- + 16 ATP \xrightarrow{\text{Nitrogenase}} 2NH_3 + H_2 + 16 ADP + 16 P_i\]

Nitrogenase enzyme: Contains two protein components:

  • Dinitrogenase reductase (Fe-protein): Contains a [4Fe-4S] cluster; transfers electrons.
  • Dinitrogenase (MoFe-protein): Contains the FeMo-cofactor (FeMoco) — the actual site of N₂ binding and reduction.

Industrial fixation (Haber process): N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃ (Fe catalyst, 450°C, 200 atm) — mimics the biological process but less efficient.

The biological process works at ambient temperature and pressure — one reason why understanding nitrogenase is so important for developing green chemistry alternatives.
Q10 Silicones — What are they? Synthesis and Structure 6½ marks ▼

Silicones (polysiloxanes) are synthetic organosilicon polymers based on a repeating Si–O–Si backbone with organic groups (typically –CH₃) attached to silicon.

General formula: (R₂SiO)ₙ
where R = organic group (usually –CH₃, –C₂H₅, or –C₆H₅)

Synthesis:

Step 1 — Preparation of chlorosilanes (Rochow Process):

Si + 2CH₃Cl →(Cu catalyst, 300°C)→ (CH₃)₂SiCl₂ (main product) Also: CH₃SiCl₃, (CH₃)₃SiCl, (CH₃)₄Si (minor products)

Step 2 — Hydrolysis of chlorosilanes:

(CH₃)₂SiCl₂ + 2H₂O → (CH₃)₂Si(OH)₂ + 2HCl (silanediol — unstable, condenses immediately)

Step 3 — Condensation polymerisation:

n (CH₃)₂Si(OH)₂ → [–Si(CH₃)₂–O–]ₙ + nH₂O (linear polysiloxane chain)

Types of Silicones (depends on functionality of chlorosilane):

Starting materialFunctionalityProduct
R₂SiCl₂BifunctionalLinear chains / oils
RSiCl₃TrifunctionalCross-linked resins / rubbers
R₃SiClMonofunctionalChain terminators (no polymer)

Properties & Uses of Silicones:

  • Chemically inert, thermally stable (−60°C to 250°C)
  • Water repellent (hydrophobic) — used as sealants, waterproofing agents
  • Low surface tension — used as lubricants, mould release agents
  • Electrically insulating — used in high-voltage insulation
  • Biocompatible — used in medical implants, contact lenses
  • Silicone rubber (cross-linked) — gaskets, O-rings, cookware
