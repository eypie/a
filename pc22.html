1. Write explanatory notes on the following:[cite: 3]
(i) Compton effect and Heisenberg's uncertainty principle [4]
(ii) Black body radiation and Planck's quantum theory [3]

(i) Compton Effect and Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle

Compton Effect: Discovered by Arthur Compton, this phenomenon occurs when X-rays or gamma rays are scattered by weakly bound electrons in a material. When an incident photon collides with an electron at rest, it transfers a part of its energy and momentum to the electron. The scattered photon therefore has lower energy, and consequently, a longer wavelength than the incident photon. This shift in wavelength (\(\Delta\lambda\)) strongly supports the particle nature of electromagnetic radiation. The equation is given by: $$\Delta\lambda = \lambda' - \lambda = \frac{h}{m_0 c}(1 - \cos\theta)$$ where \(\lambda\) is the incident wavelength, \(\lambda'\) is the scattered wavelength, \(m_0\) is the electron's rest mass, and \(\theta\) is the scattering angle.

Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle: Formulated by Werner Heisenberg, this principle states that it is fundamentally impossible to measure both the exact position and the exact momentum of a microscopic particle (like an electron) simultaneously with absolute precision. The product of their uncertainties is always greater than or equal to a constant: $$\Delta x \cdot \Delta p \ge \frac{h}{4\pi}$$ where \(\Delta x\) is the uncertainty in position and \(\Delta p\) is the uncertainty in momentum. This principle revolutionized quantum mechanics by replacing definite Bohr orbits with probabilities.

(ii) Black Body Radiation and Planck's Quantum Theory

Black Body Radiation: A perfectly black body is an idealized physical body that absorbs all incident electromagnetic radiation, regardless of frequency or angle of incidence. When heated, it also acts as a perfect emitter. The radiation emitted by a black body depends purely on its temperature. Classical physics failed to explain the intensity-wavelength distribution curve at shorter wavelengths (the "Ultraviolet Catastrophe").

Planck's Quantum Theory: To explain black body radiation, Max Planck proposed in 1900 that the emission and absorption of energy do not occur continuously, but in discrete "packets" or "quanta".

  • Energy is emitted or absorbed in integer multiples of a fundamental unit (quantum).
  • The energy (\(E\)) of each quantum is proportional to the frequency (\(\nu\)) of the radiation.
$$E = h\nu$$ where \(h\) is Planck's constant (\(6.626 \times 10^{-34} \text{ J}\cdot\text{s}\)).

2. OR: Diagram of wave function for the particle in one-dimensional box. [7][cite: 3]

Particle in a One-Dimensional Box:

Consider a particle of mass \(m\) restricted to move in a 1D box of length \(a\) (from \(x = 0\) to \(x = a\)). The potential energy inside the box is zero (\(V = 0\)), and outside it is infinite (\(V = \infty\)).

The normalized wave functions (\(\psi_n\)) obtained by solving the Schrödinger wave equation for this system are given by: $$\psi_n = \sqrt{\frac{2}{a}} \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{a}\right)$$ where \(n\) is the principal quantum number (\(n = 1, 2, 3, \dots\)).

Wave Function Diagrams:

  • For \(n=1\) (Ground State): \(\psi_1 = \sqrt{\frac{2}{a}} \sin\left(\frac{\pi x}{a}\right)\). The wave function forms a single half-wave. It is zero at the boundaries (\(x=0\) and \(x=a\)) and has a maximum amplitude at the center (\(x = a/2\)). It has zero nodes (excluding boundaries).
  • For \(n=2\) (First Excited State): \(\psi_2 = \sqrt{\frac{2}{a}} \sin\left(\frac{2\pi x}{a}\right)\). It forms two half-waves (one full sine wave) and has exactly one node at \(x = a/2\), where the probability of finding the particle is zero.
  • For \(n=3\) (Second Excited State): \(\psi_3 = \sqrt{\frac{2}{a}} \sin\left(\frac{3\pi x}{a}\right)\). It forms three half-waves and has two nodes (at \(x = a/3\) and \(x = 2a/3\)).

(In an actual exam, draw a horizontal line representing the box length \(a\). For \(n=1\), draw a single positive arch. For \(n=2\), draw one positive arch followed by a negative arch crossing the axis at the center. For \(n=3\), draw three arches crossing the axis twice.)

3. (i) Explain how molecular orbitals are formed from atomic orbitals. Apply this method to hydrogen molecule ion (\(H_2^+\)). [4]
(ii) Calculate the energies of bonding and antibonding orbitals and draw their physical representation. [3][cite: 3]

(i) Formation of Molecular Orbitals and Application to \(H_2^+\)

Molecular orbitals are formed by the Linear Combination of Atomic Orbitals (LCAO). When two atoms approach each other to form a bond, their atomic orbitals (\(\phi_A\) and \(\phi_B\)) mathematically combine (add or subtract) to form new orbitals that span the entire molecule.

  • Constructive interference (Addition): Forms a bonding molecular orbital (BMO) with lower energy.
  • Destructive interference (Subtraction): Forms an antibonding molecular orbital (ABMO) with higher energy.

Application to \(H_2^+\):
The \(H_2^+\) ion consists of two protons (nuclei A and B) and a single electron. The LCAO wave function for the molecular orbital (\(\psi\)) is: $$\psi = c_1\phi_A + c_2\phi_B$$ where \(\phi_A\) and \(\phi_B\) are the \(1s\) wave functions of the respective hydrogen atoms, and \(c_1\), \(c_2\) are mixing coefficients.

(ii) Energies and Physical Representation

Using the variation principle, the energy \(E\) is given by \(\frac{\int \psi \hat{H} \psi d\tau}{\int \psi^2 d\tau}\). Solving the secular determinant yields two energy levels:

1. Bonding Orbital Energy (\(E_+\)): Resulting from the symmetric combination (\(\psi_b = \phi_A + \phi_B\)). $$E_+ = \frac{H_{AA} + H_{AB}}{1 + S_{AB}}$$ where \(H_{AA}\) is the Coulomb integral, \(H_{AB}\) is the Resonance integral, and \(S_{AB}\) is the Overlap integral. Energy decreases, resulting in attraction.

2. Antibonding Orbital Energy (\(E_-\)): Resulting from the asymmetric combination (\(\psi_a = \phi_A - \phi_B\)). $$E_- = \frac{H_{AA} - H_{AB}}{1 - S_{AB}}$$ Energy increases, resulting in repulsion.

Physical Representation:

  • Bonding MO (\(\sigma_{1s}\)): The electron density is concentrated between the two positively charged nuclei. It forms a continuous, symmetrical electron cloud.
  • Antibonding MO (\(\sigma^*_{1s}\)): The electron density is concentrated away from the internuclear region. There is a nodal plane exactly midway between the nuclei where electron density is zero.

4. OR: What are hybrid orbitals? Calculate the wave functions for any two of \(sp\), \(sp^2\) and \(sp^3\) hybrid orbitals. [7][cite: 3]

Hybrid Orbitals:
Hybrid orbitals are a set of new, equivalent orbitals created by the mathematical mixing (linear combination) of atomic orbitals of slightly different energies (like \(s\) and \(p\)) within the same atom. This process, called hybridization, occurs during bond formation to minimize electron repulsion and maximize orbital overlap, leading to stronger bonds and defining the molecule's geometry.

1. Wave Functions of \(sp\) Hybridization:

Mixing one \(s\) orbital and one \(p\) orbital (e.g., \(p_z\)) generates two equivalent \(sp\) hybrid orbitals.
$$\psi_1 = a_1\phi_s + b_1\phi_{p_z}$$ $$\psi_2 = a_2\phi_s + b_2\phi_{p_z}$$ Since the \(s\) character is equally distributed (50%), \(a_1^2 = a_2^2 = \frac{1}{2} \implies a_1 = a_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\).
For normalization, \(a_1^2 + b_1^2 = 1 \implies b_1^2 = 1 - \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{2} \implies b_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\).
For orthogonality between \(\psi_1\) and \(\psi_2\), \(a_1a_2 + b_1b_2 = 0 \implies \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right) + \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)b_2 = 0 \implies b_2 = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\).
Final Wave functions: $$\psi_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\phi_s + \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\phi_{p_z}$$ $$\psi_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\phi_s - \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\phi_{p_z}$$

2. Wave Functions of \(sp^2\) Hybridization:

Mixing one \(s\) orbital and two \(p\) orbitals (\(p_x, p_y\)) yields three equivalent \(sp^2\) hybrid orbitals. The \(s\) character is \(1/3\).
Let \(\psi_1 = a_1\phi_s + b_1\phi_{p_x} + c_1\phi_{p_y}\). Since \(s\) is distributed equally, \(a_1 = a_2 = a_3 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\).
Assuming \(\psi_1\) lies purely along the x-axis, \(c_1 = 0\). Normalization requires \(a_1^2 + b_1^2 = 1 \implies b_1 = \sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}\).
Applying orthogonality and normalization constraints to \(\psi_2\) and \(\psi_3\), we obtain:
Final Wave functions: $$\psi_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\phi_s + \sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}\phi_{p_x}$$ $$\psi_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\phi_s - \frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}\phi_{p_x} + \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\phi_{p_y}$$ $$\psi_3 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\phi_s - \frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}\phi_{p_x} - \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\phi_{p_y}$$

5. Write and explain the qualitative relation of force constant and bond energy. How will you determine force constant. [3½+3½=7][cite: 3]

Qualitative Relation of Force Constant (\(k\)) and Bond Energy:

The force constant (\(k\)) of a chemical bond acts as a measure of the bond's stiffness or rigidity, much like the spring constant in Hooke's Law. It dictates how difficult it is to stretch or compress the bond.
Bond energy is the amount of energy required to completely break the bond holding the two atoms together.
Relation: Qualitatively, there is a direct correlation between force constant and bond energy. A higher force constant implies a stiffer, stronger bond, which in turn means that a greater amount of energy is required to dissociate the atoms. For example, a triple bond (\(C \equiv C\)) has a higher force constant and higher bond energy compared to a double bond (\(C=C\)), which is higher than a single bond (\(C-C\)).

Determination of Force Constant:

The force constant is determined experimentally using Infrared (IR) Vibrational Spectroscopy. By modeling a diatomic molecule as a simple harmonic oscillator, the fundamental vibrational frequency (\(\bar{\nu}\), expressed in wavenumbers \(\text{cm}^{-1}\)) is given by: $$\bar{\nu} = \frac{1}{2\pi c} \sqrt{\frac{k}{\mu}}$$ where:

  • \(c\) = speed of light in cm/s
  • \(k\) = force constant in dynes/cm (or N/m)
  • \(\mu = \frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\) = reduced mass of the diatomic molecule
By experimentally observing the absorption wavenumber (\(\bar{\nu}\)) from the IR spectrum and knowing the atomic masses, the equation can be rearranged to solve for \(k\): $$k = 4\pi^2 c^2 \bar{\nu}^2 \mu$$

6. OR: Discuss the importance of polarisability with respect to Raman spectrum and discuss the factors affecting it. [7][cite: 3]

Importance of Polarisability in Raman Spectrum:

In Raman spectroscopy, the fundamental mechanism relies on the inelastic scattering of light. When a molecule is placed in an oscillating electric field \(E\) of incident light, it induces a dipole moment (\(\mu_{ind}\)): $$\mu_{ind} = \alpha E$$ where \(\alpha\) is the polarisability of the molecule (a measure of how easily its electron cloud can be distorted).
Selection Rule: For a molecule to be Raman active, its polarisability must change during the molecular rotation or vibration (\(\frac{d\alpha}{dq} \neq 0\)). If the electron cloud distortion remains constant during a specific vibrational mode, that mode will not show up in the Raman spectrum. Therefore, polarisability is the central property that dictates Raman activity, just as permanent dipole moment dictates IR activity.

Factors Affecting Polarisability:

  1. Molecular Volume/Size: Larger atoms and molecules have outer electrons that are further from the nucleus. These electrons are less tightly held and can be distorted more easily by an external electric field. Hence, polarisability increases with molecular size.
  2. Number of Electrons: Molecules with a higher number of electrons generally exhibit higher polarisability because there is a larger, more diffuse electron cloud to be distorted.
  3. Nuclear Charge (Electronegativity): Strongly electronegative atoms hold their electrons very tightly, making the electron cloud rigid. Consequently, high electronegativity decreases polarisability.
  4. Nature of Bonding: Pi (\(\pi\)) bonds are more diffuse and less rigidly held than sigma (\(\sigma\)) bonds. Therefore, unsaturated molecules with double or triple bonds are more polarisable than their saturated counterparts.
7. Describe in detail the Law of Photochemistry. [6½][cite: 3]

Photochemistry is governed by two fundamental laws that describe how light interacts with matter to induce chemical change.

1. Grotthuss-Draper Law (First Law of Photochemistry):

Formulated independently by Theodor Grotthuss and John W. Draper, this law states that: "Only the light that is absorbed by a system can bring about a photochemical change."
When light falls on a substance, it can be reflected, transmitted, or absorbed. The reflected and transmitted light have no effect on the chemical bonds. Only the absorbed photons transfer energy to the molecules, causing them to enter an excited state which may subsequently lead to a chemical reaction. (Note: Not all absorbed light causes a chemical reaction; some may be lost as heat or re-emitted as fluorescence/phosphorescence).

2. Stark-Einstein Law of Photochemical Equivalence (Second Law of Photochemistry):

Proposed by Johannes Stark and Albert Einstein, this law incorporates quantum mechanics into photochemistry. It states that: "Each molecule taking part in a photochemical primary process absorbs exactly one quantum of radiation (one photon)."
Mathematically, if one mole of a substance undergoes a reaction, it must absorb one mole of photons (called an Einstein). The energy (\(E\)) of one Einstein is: $$E = N_A h \nu = \frac{N_A h c}{\lambda}$$ where \(N_A\) is Avogadro's number.
Concept of Quantum Yield (\(\Phi\)): According to this law, the primary photochemical yield should be precisely 1. However, due to secondary thermal reactions (like chain reactions causing \(\Phi > 1\)) or deactivation processes (causing \(\Phi < 1\)), the overall quantum yield of the entire reaction often deviates from unity.

8. OR: (i) What is meant by fluorescence and phosphorescence? Write their characteristics and differentiate between these. [3½]
(ii) Define specific rotation and describe method of its determination. [3][cite: 3]

(i) Fluorescence and Phosphorescence

When an electronically excited molecule loses its excess energy by emitting light, the process is called luminescence. It is divided into two types based on spin multiplicity:

Fluorescence: It is the radiative transition from the first excited singlet state to the ground singlet state (\(S_1 \to S_0\)). Since there is no change in electron spin, it is an allowed transition and occurs very rapidly.

Phosphorescence: It is the radiative transition from an excited triplet state to the ground singlet state (\(T_1 \to S_0\)). This transition requires a change in spin (spin forbidden), making it a much slower, delayed process.

Characteristic Fluorescence Phosphorescence
Lifetime Very short (\(10^{-8}\) to \(10^{-4}\) seconds). Relatively long (\(10^{-4}\) seconds to minutes/hours).
Spin State Singlet to Singlet (\(S_1 \to S_0\)). Spin conserved. Triplet to Singlet (\(T_1 \to S_0\)). Spin flipped.
Emission Duration Stops immediately when the incident light source is turned off. Continues to "glow" for some time after the light source is removed.

(ii) Specific Rotation and its Determination

Specific Rotation (\([\alpha]\)): It is an inherent property of an optically active substance. It is defined as the angle of rotation of plane-polarized light produced by a solution of concentration \(1 \text{ g/mL}\) placed in a tube of length \(1 \text{ decimeter}\) (\(10 \text{ cm}\)). $$[\alpha]_\lambda^T = \frac{\alpha_{obs}}{l \times c}$$ where \(\alpha_{obs}\) is the observed rotation angle, \(l\) is path length in dm, and \(c\) is concentration in g/mL.

Method of Determination: Specific rotation is measured using an instrument called a Polarimeter.

  1. Unpolarized light from a source (like a sodium lamp) is passed through a polarizer (e.g., Nicol prism) to yield plane-polarized light.
  2. This polarized light passes through a sample tube of known length (\(l\)) containing the optically active solution of known concentration (\(c\)).
  3. The substance rotates the plane of polarization.
  4. The light then passes through an analyzer (a second rotating prism) connected to an eyepiece. The user rotates the analyzer until the light intensity is completely extinguished (or matches a reference split field) to find the observed angle of rotation (\(\alpha_{obs}\)).
  5. The values are substituted into the formula to find the specific rotation.

9. Explain:[cite: 3]
(i) Why cannot you make icecream by keeping sweetened milk at \(0^\circ\text{C}\)? [2]
(ii) Why Salt is added in ice to make icecream? [2]
(iii) Why cannot you find the correct molecular weight of an electrolyte by the measurement of colligative properties? [2½]

(i) Making ice cream at \(0^\circ\text{C}\)

Pure water freezes exactly at \(0^\circ\text{C}\). However, sweetened milk is not pure water; it is a solution containing various dissolved non-volatile solutes like sugar, proteins, and salts. According to the colligative property known as depression in freezing point, adding a solute lowers the freezing point of the solvent. Therefore, the freezing point of sweetened milk is significantly below \(0^\circ\text{C}\). Keeping it at exactly \(0^\circ\text{C}\) will leave it in a liquid state, meaning it will not freeze into ice cream.

(ii) Adding salt to ice

To freeze the sweetened milk (which requires a temperature lower than \(0^\circ\text{C}\)), a surrounding cooling medium must reach that lower temperature. When salt (like NaCl) is mixed with ice, it creates a freezing mixture. The dissolution of salt lowers the freezing point of the ice-water equilibrium, forcing the ice to melt. Melting is an endothermic process, drawing heat from the surroundings (the ice cream mixture). This drives the temperature of the ice-salt mixture down significantly (e.g., to \(-21^\circ\text{C}\) for NaCl), providing a cold enough environment to freeze the ice cream.

(iii) Incorrect molecular weight of electrolytes

Colligative properties (like osmotic pressure, relative lowering of vapor pressure, etc.) depend strictly on the number of solute particles present in the solution, not their identity. When an electrolyte (like NaCl or \(\text{K}_2\text{SO}_4\)) dissolves in water, it dissociates into multiple ions. This increases the total number of particles compared to what would be expected from the formula weight. Because the measured colligative property is unusually high due to the extra particles, the calculated molecular weight (\(M = K \cdot \text{Weight} / \Delta T\)) will appear unusually low (or abnormal). The deviation is corrected using the Van't Hoff factor (\(i\)).

10. OR: What is molal depression constant? Describe any one method to determine molecular weight. [3½+3½=7][cite: 3]

Molal Depression Constant (\(K_f\)):

Also known as the cryoscopic constant, it is defined as the depression in the freezing point (\(\Delta T_f\)) produced when exactly \(1 \text{ mole}\) of a non-volatile, non-electrolyte solute is dissolved in \(1 \text{ kg}\) (\(1000 \text{ g}\)) of the solvent. It is mathematically derived from the freezing point depression equation: $$\Delta T_f = K_f \cdot m$$ If molality \(m = 1\), then \(\Delta T_f = K_f\). Its unit is \(\text{K}\cdot\text{kg}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}\).

Method to Determine Molecular Weight (Rast's Camphor Method):

This is a standard cryoscopic method used to determine the molecular weight of an unknown solid solute, utilizing camphor as the solvent. Camphor is chosen because it possesses an exceptionally high molal depression constant (\(K_f \approx 39.7 \text{ K}\cdot\text{kg/mol}\)), which produces a very large, easily measurable drop in freezing point even for small amounts of solute.

Procedure:

  1. The precise melting point of pure camphor (\(T_1\)) is measured using a capillary tube melting point apparatus.
  2. A carefully weighed mass of the unknown solid solute (\(W_B\)) is intimately mixed with a known mass of camphor (\(W_A\)). The mixture is melted to form a homogeneous solution and then cooled.
  3. The melting point of the solid mixture (\(T_2\)) is then measured.
  4. The depression in freezing/melting point is calculated: \(\Delta T_f = T_1 - T_2\).
Calculation: By rearranging the molality formula, the molecular weight of the solute (\(M_B\)) is calculated as: $$M_B = \frac{K_f \times W_B \times 1000}{\Delta T_f \times W_A}$$
